# International master teachers and high-performing systems: classroom techniques by age band

Research notes for the live-agent teacher persona (one Chinese K-12 student, live video + text board, lessons of 3-5 sections with board points, short spoken lines and one exercise per section). Access date for every source: **2026-09-30**. Source ids (S1...) resolve in the register at the end. Labels used throughout: **[documented practice]** = a classroom move described in a primary or evaluative source; **[award rhetoric]** = citation language that does not describe a classroom move; **[inference]** = my reading; **[snippet only]** = seen only in a search-result excerpt, page not fetched; **not verified** = could not be confirmed from a source in this session.

Method note: 9 web searches (budget then exhausted) and ~70 page fetches; five PDFs were converted locally with pypdf (NCETM Five Big Ideas PDF failed to extract and was replaced by the HTML page; Sutton Trust, NRICH/NCETM Shanghai report, Takahashi 2006/2008, Boylan et al. 2019, Lepik et al. 2015 and the Singapore 2007 release were extracted successfully).

## KQ1. Global Teacher Prize (Varkey Foundation) winners 2015-2026: who they are and what practice the award actually documents

### Takeaway
The prize citations are dominated by community impact and biography; concrete classroom technique is documented for only about half the winners (Atwell, Al Hroub, Zafirakou, Disale, Thorpe, Al Mansour) and even there at the level of "approach" (choice-based reading workshop, play-based conflict reduction, home-language greetings, QR-linked textbooks, culturally relevant curriculum, project-based learning), not lesson mechanics. The recurring documented move is relationship-first teaching adapted to the learner's home culture and language.

### Cited Findings
Winners by year (the Wikipedia list and the Varkey page disagree on the label for Al Mansour: Wikipedia lists him as the 2025 winner, Varkey's own page lists him under 2024 and Rouble Nagi under 2026; no 2022 winner appears in either list) — [S1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Teacher_Prize); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize).

- **2015 Nancie Atwell (USA), Center for Teaching and Learning, Edgecomb, Maine; middle-school English (ages ~11-14).** [documented practice] "students read an average of 40 books a year, choose which books they read, and write prolifically"; reading and writing workshop with mini-lessons and conferences, set out in her book *In the Middle* (1987); the school is "a demonstration school for developing and disseminating teaching methods". Varkey citation language: "pioneering work in literacy and her student-centered approach" [award rhetoric]. — [S1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Teacher_Prize); [S5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancie_Atwell); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2016 Hanan Al Hroub (Palestine), primary-age children in refugee-camp schools near Bethlehem.** [documented practice] Play-based method written up in her book *We Play and Learn*; "uses play that is aimed at resolving violence and tension"; slogan "No to Violence"; reported that "the violence that kids were seeing in the streets, they were carrying into the classroom" and that "after a few months, their behavior and attitude improved dramatically". Varkey: "innovative approach to education focused on non-violence and peace". — [S6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanan_Al_Hroub); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2017 Maggie MacDonnell (Canada), Salluit, Nunavik (Inuit village); secondary, boys 13-18 and "especially girls".** [documented practice, but outside the lesson] "sports and fitness programs as tools for building community cohesion"; "life skills and social awareness programs"; girls' programmes "linked to up to a 500% increase in the enrollment of girls" (Wikipedia figure; not verified against Varkey). — [S7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maggie_MacDonnell)
- **2018 Andria Zafirakou (UK), Alperton Community School, Brent, London; secondary art and textiles.** [documented practice] "taught herself the basic phrases used by children in about 35 of them [home languages] - including Hindi, Gujarati and Tamil"; used art "as a language-free method of communication", introducing pupils to art of their own culture before Western movements; "organised extra lessons during the day and the weekend, including giving pupils a quiet place to work"; introduced wellbeing and yoga classes; made the curriculum "more applied rather than theoretical". Her stated rule: "Build the relationship, build that trust. And then everything else can happen." — [S8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andria_Zafirakou); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2019 Peter Tabichi (Kenya), Keriko Mixed Day Secondary School, Pwani village, Nakuru; maths and physics; student-teacher ratio 58:1; one computer, intermittent internet.** [documented practice, mostly extra-curricular] "launched the Talent Nurturing Club, which has dramatically improved attendance"; a Peace Club; students entered science competitions (Intel ISEF finals, Royal Society of Chemistry award); students "work on renewable energy and devices which can support people with disabilities"; "donates 80% of his salary"; "doubled the number of students attending university". Varkey citation stresses the salary donation and competitions. — [S9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Tabichi); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2020 Ranjitsinh Disale (India), Zilla Parishad Primary School, Paritewadi, Solapur; primary.** [documented practice] "adding QR codes to primary category books so that his students can get links to audio poems, video lectures, assignments and stories" (later adopted by ~300 schools); "demonstrating scientific experiments from the science lab built by him"; girls' attendance reported at 100%. Textbook translation into Kannada and the "Let's Cross the Borders" project were **not** confirmed in the fetched page (not verified). — [S10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranjitsinh_Disale); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2021 Keishia Thorpe (USA), Prince George's County, Maryland; 12th-grade English.** [documented practice, curriculum level] "redesigning the 12th grade English curriculum to make it more relevant to immigrants and refugees"; Varkey: "Opened up college education for low-income, first-generation American, immigrant and refugee students". Reading-gain percentages often quoted in press were **not** found in the fetched sources (not verified). — [S11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keishia_Thorpe); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2023 Sister Zeph (Pakistan), Zephaniah Women's Education and Empowerment Foundation, Gujranwala; kindergarten to grade 10 plus afternoon classes.** [documented practice] no corporal punishment; affection as a deliberate stance: "when a teacher shows such affection to the children, they feel protected... they are just with another mother"; many teachers are former students. — [S12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_Zeph); [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize)
- **2024/2025 Mansour Al Mansour (Saudi Arabia), Prince Saud bin Jalawi School, al-Ahsa; subject/grade not stated on the prize page.** [documented practice, approach level] "connects education to real-world issues such as climate change, financial literacy, and social responsibility through project-based learning"; "tailors his methods to meet individual student needs, actively engages families in learning"; an "innovative educational platform" using AI/VR; students reached Regeneron ISEF; "over 5,000 volunteer hours". — [S3](https://globalteacherprize.org/pages/mansour-al-mansour)
- **2026 Rouble Nagi (India).** [snippet only] art-based education; "more than 800 learning centres across over 100 underserved communities"; "interactive educational murals" ("living walls of learning") teaching literacy, numeracy and health. — [S2](https://varkeyfoundation.org/global-teacher-prize); [S4](https://teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/rouble-nagi-from-india-wins-the-2026-global-teacher-prize)

### Inferences
- The prize rewards *access and belonging* moves more than instructional mechanics; the transferable classroom techniques are: (a) greet/anchor in the learner's own language and culture before content (Zafirakou); (b) reading volume with student choice plus short mini-lessons (Atwell); (c) low-stakes play and games to lower affect before academic work in ages 6-8 (Al Hroub); (d) attach audio/video/assignment links to the textbook so the learner can replay (Disale); (e) real-world project framing for teens (Al Mansour, Tabichi).
- Winners who teach 16-18 are cited for *curriculum relevance and college access*; winners who teach 6-11 are cited for *safety, play and attendance*. This is consistent with the age-band split in KQ8.

### Gaps
- Varkey's own winner pages for 2015-2023 returned 404 at the `/pages/<name>` pattern; practice descriptions come from Wikipedia summaries that cite Varkey press and newspapers. Lesson-level detail (questioning, board, feedback) is absent for every winner.
- The 2022 gap year and the 2024-vs-2025 label for Al Mansour are unresolved between S1 and S2.

## KQ3. Lemov (Teach Like a Champion / Uncommon Schools), Coe et al. (Sutton Trust "What makes great teaching?"), Berliner and Hattie on expert teachers

### Takeaway
Lemov's catalogue (63 techniques in the 2021 third edition, distilled from video of high-performing teachers) supplies the most granular, testable moves — cold call, wait time, no opt out, everybody writes, exemplar planning, retrieval practice. Coe et al. (2014) rank pedagogical content knowledge and quality of instruction (Rosenshine's ten principles) as the only strongly evidenced components and explicitly list lavish praise, discovery-first, ability grouping, re-reading and learning styles as unsupported. Berliner/Hattie describe experts as automatic on routine so they can notice and respond, deeply representing the subject, monitoring and giving feedback, and pushing past surface goals.

### Cited Findings
**Lemov / Teach Like a Champion**
- TLAC 3.0 contains "63" techniques; "Upon studying videos of high-performing teachers, Lemov distills their methods into concrete tangible actions for others to use and adapt"; the book covers checking for understanding, raising academic expectations, increasing the student's cognitive "ratio", engagement, writing-intensive classrooms, discussion, and positive behaviour redirection. — [S45](https://teachlikeachampion.org/books/teach-like-a-champion-3-0/)
- Technique numbering in 3.0 (Technique 15 No Opt Out, 33 Wait Time, 34 Cold Call) and one-line definitions — Cold Call: "asking students questions regardless of whether they have their hand raised"; No Opt Out: "don't accept 'I don't know' as the final answer"; Wait Time: "sufficient time for students to formulate responses" [snippet only]. — [S47](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-teach-like-a-champion/)
- Ten techniques new or revised in 3.0, as Lemov described them to TES [documented practice]: **Habits of Attention** (students build "internal habits to focus their own attention" — eye contact, nodding when peers speak); **Exemplar Planning** (write the exemplar answer before the lesson: "if I take a minute to write this out... it's easier to remember"); **Delivery Moves** (decide in advance whether a question will be answered by partner talk, cold call or writing, and say so); **Phrasing Fundamentals** (avoid obvious questions; "The importance of questioning is often overlooked"); **Everybody Writes** ("One of the main purposes of writing is to decide what I think, not justify what I think"; soften with "might"); **Silent Solo** (brief individual writing before discussion, launched with a one-word cue such as "go"); **Private Individual Correction** (correct quietly, "framing expectations around purpose rather than power"); **Phase Reading** (teacher-led read-aloud in every subject for fluency and "shared emotional experience"); **Retrieval Practice** (systematic recall via cold call, vocabulary "in different ways and in new settings"); **Knowledge Organisers** (one-page key terms/figures/timelines quizzed "several times a week"). — [S46](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/10-new-teaching-techniques-doug-lemov-wants-you-know)
- Techniques commonly attributed to earlier editions (Right is Right, Stretch It, Show Call, Culture of Error, Do Now, Exit Ticket, Turn and Talk, Positive Framing, Warm/Strict, 100 Percent, Format Matters) were **not verified** from a fetched source this session (Wiley, O'Reilly, Akademika and Wikipedia pages all blocked or 404); treat as book knowledge.

**Coe, Aloisi, Higgins & Major (2014), Sutton Trust / Durham CEM**
- Six components ranked by evidence: "(Pedagogical) content knowledge (Strong evidence...)" — "teachers must also understand the ways students think about the content, be able to evaluate the thinking behind students' own methods, and identify students' common misconceptions"; "Quality of instruction (Strong evidence...)" — "effective questioning and use of assessment... reviewing previous learning, providing model responses for students, giving adequate time for practice to embed skills securely and progressively introducing new learning (scaffolding)"; "Classroom climate (Moderate...)" — "a classroom that is constantly demanding more, but still recognising students' self-worth... attributing student success to effort rather than ability"; "Classroom management (Moderate...)" — "efficient use of lesson time... clear rules that are consistently enforced"; teacher beliefs and professional behaviours (some evidence). — [S28](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf); [S29](https://www.cem.org/blog/6-elements-of-great-teaching)
- Rosenshine's ten principles as reproduced in the report: "1. Begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning 2. Present new material in small steps, with student practice after each step 3. Ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students 4. Provide models for problem solving and worked examples 5. Guide student practice 6. Check for student understanding 7. Obtain a high success rate 8. Provide scaffolds for difficult tasks 9. Require and monitor independent practice 10. Engage students in weekly and monthly review". — [S28](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)
- Practices "whose use is not supported by research evidence": "Use praise lavishly" ("Praise for successful performance on an easy task can be interpreted by a student as evidence that the teacher has a low perception of his or her ability"); "Allow learners to discover key ideas for themselves" ("if teachers want them to learn new ideas, knowledge or methods they need to teach them directly"); "Group learners by ability"; "Encourage re-reading and highlighting"; "Address issues of confidence and low aspirations before you try to teach content" ("Start getting them to succeed and their motivation and confidence should increase"); learning styles ("no benefits"); "Ensure learners are always active" ("Memory is the residue of thought"). — [S28](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)

**Berliner / Hattie on expert teachers**
- Berliner (2001), *Int. J. Educational Research* 35(5), 463-482: expertise in teaching is context-bound and culturally variable; distinguishes "the good teacher and the successful teacher"; stresses "adaptive or fluid expertise, automaticity and flexibility"; development of expertise "is seen as an increase in agency over time". — [S31](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/learning-about-and-learning-from-expert-teachers)
- Berliner (2001, p. 471): "There is no basis to believe there are differences in the sophistication of the cognitive processes used by teachers and experts in other fields"; experts "rely on contextualized thinking", "respond quickly and intuitively", and share "fast and accurate pattern recognition capabilities" (Endsley's situation awareness). — [S30](https://ger.mercy.edu/index.php/ger/article/view/108/79)
- Berliner (2004), "Describing the Behavior and Documenting the Accomplishments of Expert Teachers" (title and year verified; body not accessible). Secondary summaries [snippet only] list: automaticity for routine tasks, processing many cues simultaneously, recognising what is atypical, being "opportunistic and flexible", monitoring learning and responding to individuals, making predictions about lessons. — [S32](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/0270467604265535); [S34](https://blogs.nottingham.ac.uk/primaryeducationnetwork/?p=5552)
- Hattie (2003), "Teachers Make a Difference": expert teachers "can identify essential representations of their subject, can guide learning through classroom interactions, can monitor learning and provide feedback, can attend to affective attributes, and can influence student outcomes" (five dimensions, 16 attributes); experts "optimise their automaticity to allow them to be able to concentrate on and respond to more complex, i.e., less routine, challenges"; they "transcend surface learning and encourage deep learning". — [S30](https://ger.mercy.edu/index.php/ger/article/view/108/79); [S33](https://research.acer.edu.au/research_conference_2003/4/)
- Teacher-noticing reviews [snippet only]: experts "focused more on students and important content-related aspects", noticed "issues concerning simultaneity, multidimensionality, and immediacy at higher levels than novice teachers". — König et al. 2022 review (search snippet; not fetched)

### Inferences
- For an AI teacher the "automaticity" finding translates into scripting the routine layer (greeting, review, board template, exercise hand-off) so that the model's live attention goes to the student's answer and its errors, which is exactly where Coe's PCK component and Hattie's "monitor and feedback" dimension say experts differ.
- Coe's "not supported" list is a direct veto list: no undifferentiated praise, no discovery-first on new content, no learning-style talk, no "build confidence first" preambles.

### Gaps
- Full TLAC technique list and definitions could not be fetched (publisher pages blocked); only the 10 TES-described techniques and three snippet-defined ones are sourced.
- Berliner 2004 full text and the Nottingham summary page were not retrievable; propositions are from a search snippet.

## KQ4. Japanese practice: lesson study, structured problem solving (hatsumon → kikan-shido → neriage → matome), bansho, and the TIMSS video evidence

### Takeaway
The Japanese primary maths lesson is a single problem, a single objective, and a fixed arc: present the problem without demonstrating (hatsumon), let students estimate/plan (mitoshi) and work while the teacher circulates and notes who used which method (kikan-shido), then a long comparison discussion of several methods ordered from most common to most different (neriage), then a teacher summary (matome). The board (bansho) is never erased so that the finished board is the record of the lesson's reasoning. TIMSS video (1995) quantifies the contrast: Japanese grade-8 students spent 44% of time inventing solutions and 41% practising routines versus 90-96% practising in the US and Germany; 53% of Japanese lessons contained proofs versus 0% in the US.

### Cited Findings
- **Typical flow (Takahashi 2006, APEC slides).** US: "Demonstrates a procedure → Assigned similar problems to students as exercises → Homework assignment". Japan: "Presents a problem to the students without first demonstrating how to solve the problem → Individual or group problem solving → Compare and discuss multiple solution methods → Summary, exercises and homework assignment". "Each lesson is designed around solving a single problem to achieve a single objective in a topic." Three characteristics of structured problem solving: "Carefully selected word problems and activities, and their cohesiveness"; "Extensive discussion (Neriage)"; "Emphasis on blackboard practice (Bansho)". Bansho is "To compare, contrast, and discuss ideas that students present" and "To help to organize student thinking and discovery of new ideas". Teachers "need a clear plan for the discussion... which will anticipate the variety of solution methods". Stigler & Hiebert's label "structured problem solving" is quoted. — [S37](https://www.criced.tsukuba.ac.jp/math/apec/apec2006/pdf/takahashi_ppt_1.pdf)
- **Phases (Batteau, Miyakawa & Ryu 2025, *Educational Studies in Mathematics*).** Hatsumon = "presentation of the problem of the day, eventually followed by the mitoshi" (mitoshi = "estimation, planning, or anticipation of the solution"); kikan-shido = "instruction at students' desks. Individual student work, eventually followed by group work"; neriage = "whole-class discussion on students' ideas, strategies, and/or solutions"; matome = "summary by the teacher, eventually followed by further development". Observed Lesson 1 (32 min): hatsumon 0:00-6:27, individual work 6:27-7:45, neriage 7:45-21:20, group work 21:20-30:00, matome 30:00-32:00. Board written "progressively without erasing": unit title ("feeling the lengths"), the main question ("How long is the length of the corridor in the school?"), students' estimates ("50 m", "35 m", "60 m", "125 m", "1 or 2 km"), the measuring methods; the final board is "a comprehensive record of the lesson's structure". Teacher lines: "What is the reason for your estimation?"; "How can you measure the length of the corridor?"; "Why use a tape measure?"; estimates were sorted into intervals ("less than 35, between 35 and 60...") to structure comparison. Cites Stigler & Hiebert 1999, Takahashi 2006/2008, Shimizu 1999/2009 (lesson as drama), Fujii 2018. — [S39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12145323/)
- **Neriage mechanics (Takahashi 2008, ICME-11).** "Japanese teachers usually do not tell students how to solve a problem before students try to solve the problem by themselves"; neriage (from *neriageru*, "to polish up") is "the heart of the lesson"; "the solving of the problem by each student at the beginning of the lesson is a preparation for Neriage"; during work time "teachers often use a seating chart of the class to jot down how each student approaches the problem as well as to plan how to lead the discussion"; the teacher "might ask one of the students who used the most common method to share his/her method to begin the discussion, and then ask another student to share different methods"; at that stage teachers "usually do not say that the answers are right or wrong"; "Teachers carefully use blackboard writing for students to see all the solution methods from their peers"; neriage then asks for "common ideas or approaches... or some differences", then "whether each approach has advantages and limitations", and the textbook follows with a problem (600 m² vs 900 m² farms) where the common-multiple method becomes impractical so that division-to-unit-rate is seen as better. Worked example: grade-5 "Which cabin is the most crowded?" with four anticipated methods (people per m², m² per person, common multiple of area, common multiple of people). — [S38](https://lessonresearch.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Characteristics-of-Japanese-Mathematics-Lessons-2.pdf)
- **TIMSS 1995 Video Study (NCES).** 231 grade-8 lessons ("100 in Germany, 50 in Japan, and 81 in the United States"). US and Germany: "In the acquisition phase, the teacher demonstrates and/or explains how to solve an example problem", then "an application phase, students practice"; Japan: "Problem solving comes first, followed by a time in which students reflect on the problem, share the solution methods they have generated, and jointly work to develop explicit understandings". Time practising routine procedures: "41 percent in Japan" vs "96 percent... in Germany and 90 percent in the United States"; "Japanese students spend 44 percent of their time inventing new solutions"; proofs: "None of the U.S. lessons include proofs, whereas 10 percent of German lessons and 53 percent of Japanese lessons include proofs"; "More than three-fourths of German and Japanese teachers develop concepts when they include them in their lessons, compared with about one-fifth of U.S. teachers"; "U.S. lessons are less coherent than Japanese lessons" and "more frequently interrupted". — [S35](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs99/timssvid/index.asp)
- Hiebert on the same study (press): "Mathematical ideas are difficult. They require struggling"; US teachers "want to jump in and prevent confusion"; in Japan "Confusion is an expected part of what (students) are going to confront"; the piece contrasts roughly 40 seconds before a US teacher intervenes with about 15 minutes of independent work in Japan (figures as reported by the journalist). — [S36](https://apmreports.org/episode/2015/08/27/an-american-way-of-teaching)
- Lesson study: Japanese teachers, researchers and administrators "worked collaboratively through Lesson Study" to build problem-solving lessons from Polya's four phases (translated 1954) and the 1977 open-ended approach; textbooks now begin "almost every chapter" with a problem. — [S38](https://lessonresearch.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Characteristics-of-Japanese-Mathematics-Lessons-2.pdf); [S37](https://www.criced.tsukuba.ac.jp/math/apec/apec2006/pdf/takahashi_ppt_1.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a one-student live lesson the "neriage" comparison cannot be between peers, so the AI must *supply* the alternative methods it anticipated (Takahashi's anticipation list) and ask the student to compare its own method with them — "same idea or different idea? which breaks first when the numbers grow?"
- Bansho maps directly onto the text board: one panel per lesson, top-left problem, middle = student's attempt(s) verbatim, right = summary; never clear the panel mid-lesson (Chinese equivalent: 板书设计, the planned board layout that Chinese teachers are also assessed on).
- The 32-minute lesson with a 6-minute problem set-up and 14-minute discussion is a usable tempo template for ages 9-11; the TIMSS "invent first" pattern is documented for age 13-14.

### Gaps
- No open-access paper specifically on bansho conventions (left-to-right flow, colour, name cards) was retrievable (JMTE and Springer pages blocked); the not-erasing convention and layout come from Batteau et al. 2025 and Takahashi 2006.
- Stigler & Hiebert's own five-step Japanese script ("reviewing the previous lesson... highlighting and summarizing the major point") could not be fetched verbatim (TERC and MAA pages down); the structure above is from Takahashi and NCES.

## KQ5. Shanghai and Singapore "mastery" as exported to England (NCETM), and Finnish practice claims

### Takeaway
What English observers documented in Shanghai-taught lessons is very specific: 35-minute lessons on one idea, every question and follow-up pre-planned, whole-class interactive teaching with no differentiation of content, precise full-sentence mathematical language and stem sentences repeated across contexts, concrete and abstract shown together, procedural and conceptual variation ("small changes"), whole-class repetition of a newly built rule, a deeper task for fast finishers, and same-day intervention. NCETM codified this as the Five Big Ideas. The DfE's own evaluation found no attainment effect of the exchange alone and only exploratory KS1 gains in high-implementation schools. Finnish "practice" claims are thin: the documented Finnish classroom is textbook-driven whole-class teaching, not a distinctive pedagogy.

### Cited Findings
**NCETM Five Big Ideas (official curriculum body)** — [S25](https://www.ncetm.org.uk/teaching-for-mastery/mastery-explained/five-big-ideas-in-teaching-for-mastery/)
- Coherence: "Teaching is designed to enable a coherent learning progression through the curriculum, providing access for all pupils to develop a deep and connected understanding of mathematics".
- Representation and Structure: "Teachers carefully select representations of mathematics to expose mathematical structure. The intention is to support pupils in 'seeing' the mathematics".
- Mathematical Thinking: "looking for patterns and relationships, making connections, conjecturing, reasoning, and generalising".
- Fluency: "Efficient, accurate recall of key number facts and procedures is essential for fluency, freeing pupils' minds to think deeply".
- Variation: "draw closer attention to a key feature of a mathematical concept or structure through varying some elements while keeping others constant"; two kinds, conceptual (varying representations) and procedural (varying the learning sequence).
- Stem sentences, ping-pong questioning and concrete-pictorial-abstract are NCETM practitioner vocabulary; they were **not** found verbatim on the Five Big Ideas page (the Feb-2024 PDF did not extract) — not verified as NCETM wording this session.

**NCETM/NRICH Shanghai mid-exchange report, Nov 2014 (29 Shanghai teachers in 22 English primaries)** — [S24](https://nrich.maths.org/content/id/10757/Shanghai_teachers_special_report_Nov_2014.pdf)
- "Each lesson is designed in minute detail; each step choreographed; each question planned meticulously, and follow-up questions, according to a pupil's first response, also planned."
- "The textbook is a constant component of the teacher's daily routine... One Shanghai teacher referred to it as 'his reference book.'"
- "a strong and ever-present emphasis on getting children to use words and sentences precisely to describe everyday situations that contain mathematical events. ('The bus starts its journey with four passengers; then at a bus stop two more get on, so the bus now has six passengers')."
- Simple content "from a variety of angles, and at great length"; "One lesson connects to the next in the 'step by step approach' with only small changes made (variation)"; "High expectations of speaking in full sentences and mathematical language e.g - addend + addend = sum"; "Construction of 'rules' (generalities) with whole class repetition."
- Times tables taught as "tens first, then connect the fives, then 2, 4 and 8", i.e. patterns not rote.
- For mixed prior attainment: "mid lesson - a deeper task (the Chinese use the phrase Dong Now Ching [动脑筋]) being given to those who've shown speedy grasp... This is not acceleration - but deepening."
- Teacher quotes: "same day intervention has had in preventing the gap and ensuring learners begin the next day at the same starting point"; "the variety of concepts taught... within 35 minutes"; "nothing is assumed, everything is taught explicitly"; "The children are becoming more precise in the language they use".
- Y4 fractions lesson: map of China → "China is the whole and Shanghai is a part" → Europe/England → school → journey (length) → shape (area) → 6 swans (quantity), "For every context the same sentence was used. The whole is... a part is..."; an error ("the school is the whole and Year 4 is a part") is corrected because "the whole and the parts need to be the same noun".
- Y2 subtraction lesson: picture story (4 children in a car, 1 gets out) → say it in words → write 4 − 1 = 3 → "what the 4 represents, what the 1 represents and what the 3 represents"; "the concrete and the abstract to be represented together"; terms "At First", "Middle", "Now" for minuend/subtrahend/difference; unknown placed in each position in turn; the 4 − 0 = 4 misconception ("everyone got out and then everyone got back in") handled by writing the child's own story on the board step by step as 4 − 4 + 4.

**NCETM secondary blog on the 2015 Shanghai secondary exchange** — [S26](https://www.ncetm.org.uk/features/teaching-mathematics-for-mastery-at-secondary-school)
- "Whole class teaching, with no differentiation of activities or content: all pupils engaged with the same mathematical ideas"; "structured teacher input (5 minutes or so at a time), interspersed with purposeful pupil activity on tasks employing procedural variation"; "short, precise steps with clear logical progression"; "concentrating on one key mathematical idea"; "insisting that pupils use precise mathematical language - e.g. 'product', 'quotient', 'numerator', 'denominator', 'reciprocal'"; "deep questioning, not just focused on 'getting the answer' but also requiring the pupils to articulate how they worked out"; "results generalised algebraically".

**DfE longitudinal evaluation (Boylan et al., Sheffield Hallam, Jan 2019)** — [S27](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5c49b38340f0b61717193d2d/MTE_main_report.pdf)
- Table 2, Shanghai vs England classroom practice: Shanghai = "Whole-class interactive teaching, brisk tempo to cover multiple small steps, focus on questioning, mini-plenaries, teaching for variation, mathematical talk as an instructional priority, emphasis on correct mathematical language"; England = "Explanation through teacher transmission (quick pace) plus individual group practice (slower pace), start from objectives, plenary at end of lesson if at all."
- Shanghai primary teachers teach "two 35 minutes lessons per day" (some three); lessons "commencing from a problem rather than lesson objectives"; "Pupil talk is an instructional priority"; "careful choice of examples and precise mathematical language (Gu, Huang, & Marton, 2004)"; "daily intervention is used to support those needing extra tuition" so "pupils progress together".
- Impact: "Analyses of impact of participation in the MTE alone do not indicate any effect on attainment, either at KS1 or at KS2" (para 23); exploratory analysis "found positive impacts on pupil KS1 mathematics attainment in those schools most directly involved in the MTE exchange. However, there is no quantifiable evidence... [for] KS2" (para 49); "The findings for the MTE are inconclusive" (para 50).
- Related observational claim [snippet only]: "whole class interaction" used 72% of the time in Chinese classrooms vs 24% in England. — [S56](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/whole-class-interaction-is-key-to-china-maths-success)

**Finland**
- Lepik, Grevholm & Viholainen (2015), *Nordic Studies in Mathematics Education* 20(3-4), survey of 400+ teachers: "In Finland the textbook is the crucial resource for exercises"; "Almost 45 % of the teachers use the textbook simply as an exercise book"; Finnish teachers "see the textbook as a concretized curriculum that... prescribes the content, as well as the goals and didactical solutions for teaching (Mikkilä-Erdmann, Olkinuora & Mattila, 2009)"; Perkkilä (2002): "teachers' attempts to cover the whole textbook in a limited time frame caused a feeling of urgency"; 50% of Finnish teachers chose their own textbook. — [S48](https://ncm.gu.se/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/20_34_129156_lepik.pdf)
- Andrews et al. (2014, *Educational Studies in Mathematics*, "PISA, TIMSS and Finnish mathematics teaching: an enigma in search of an explanation") [snippet only]: video studies show Finnish practice "rather traditional" with "substantial whole-class interaction". — [S49](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10649-014-9545-3)

### Inferences
- The Shanghai package is the closest documented match to the agent's format (one idea, ~35 minutes, planned questions, board that pairs concrete and abstract, stem sentence, small variation, exercise after each step). Its documented weakness is evidential: the DfE evaluation could not show attainment gains from the exchange alone, so the moves are "documented practice of a high-performing system", not proven transfer.
- Finland should not be presented as a source of distinctive lesson technique; its documented classroom is textbook-led whole-class teaching, so claims like "Finnish teachers don't use textbooks/tests" should be labelled unsupported.

### Gaps
- NCETM's Feb-2024 Five Big Ideas PDF could not be text-extracted; "stem sentences", "ping-pong" and "CPA" as NCETM wording remain not verified.
- Singapore's own mastery/CPA documentation (MOE syllabus) was not fetched; Singapore enters here only via the England import and the PAT citations.

## KQ6. Engelmann's Direct Instruction and Project Follow Through

### Takeaway
DI is the most fully specified technique base in the set: field-tested scripts, examples and non-examples juxtaposed to make one interpretation possible ("faultless communication"), 10-14 unison responses per minute on a signal, immediate correction, 90% review / 10% new, placement by skill. Project Follow Through (1968-, K-3, high-poverty sites) ranked DI first in reading, maths, spelling and language and positive on affective measures; later meta-analyses put DI's effect at ~0.6 SD.

### Cited Findings
- Delivery, as summarised by Athabasca University: "scripts that contains carefully sequenced instruction, questions and prompts. These scripts have been field tested"; children "grouped together for the lesson on the basis of ability, are seated in a semi-circle"; "learners are asked to respond as a group, giving their responses in unison at the signal of the teacher"; "The pace is fast - very fast" with "a rate of 10 to 14 responses per minute"; "children are busy responding to examples and non-examples presented at a high rate"; "errors are corrected immediately"; motto "Teach more in less time." — [S41](https://psyc.athabascau.ca/open/engelmann/direct.php)
- Faultless communication: DI design aims "to reduce ambiguity for students and to achieve what is often referred to in the literature as faultless communication"; designers "Select appropriate examples (introductory examples for initial practice and discrimination or non-examples for practice over time)"; programmes "are scripted, incorporate choral responding, use positive feedback and provide recommendations for systematically correcting errors"; choral responding is "an effective and surprisingly enjoyable delivery technique for increasing student engagement". — [S44](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/the-case-for-direct-instruction/)
- NIFDI principles: "All children can be taught"; "Low performers and disadvantaged learners must be taught at a faster rate than typically occurs if they are to catch up"; "All details of instruction must be controlled to minimize the chance of students misinterpreting the information being taught and to maximize the reinforcing effect of instruction"; placement by skill level, gradual skill introduction, mastery-based progression, field testing before publication. The slogan "if the student hasn't learned the teacher hasn't taught" does **not** appear on the NIFDI page (not verified as NIFDI wording). — [S42](https://www.nifdi.org/what-is-di/basic-philosophy)
- Structure: "I do / We do / You do"; "only 10% of the material is new, while the remaining 90% is a review of previously taught content". — [S43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_instruction)
- Project Follow Through: begun under Johnson (1967/68); scale reported inconsistently — "over 700,000 children in 170 disadvantaged communities" (Athabasca citing Coombs 1998) versus "over 200,000 children" (Stein & Rolf) — note the conflict; DI "was implemented at 19 sites"; "Direct Instruction was first in reading, math, spelling and language"; Behavior Analysis was "the only" other model with "across-the-board positive effects"; DI "was ranked first in achievement for poor students, students who were not poor, urban students, rural students, African American students, Hispanic students, and Native American students"; DI students "also demonstrated higher self-esteem and self-confidence, as indicated by agreed-upon affective measures". — [S40](https://psyc.athabascau.ca/open/engelmann/direct-evid.php); [S43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_instruction); [S44](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/the-case-for-direct-instruction/)
- Later evidence: Stockard et al. (2018), 318 studies, "statistically significant positive effects... in reading, mathematics, language and spelling", average "approximately 0.6 standard deviations"; Hattie's synthesis "average effect size was 0.59". — [S43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_instruction); [S44](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/the-case-for-direct-instruction/)

### Inferences
- DI and Japanese structured problem solving are the two poles the agent must choose between per section: DI-style "faultless" example sequences for new procedures and facts (especially ages 6-8 and for weak prerequisites), problem-first struggle for concepts the student can reach with prior knowledge (ages 9+). Coe's veto on discovery-first for *new* content and the TIMSS "invent first" evidence are reconciled if the problem-first phase is short and the teacher-led comparison/summary is mandatory.
- The 10-14 responses/minute rate is a design number for the youngest band: short spoken prompts, one-word or one-number answers, signal, correct, repeat.

### Gaps
- Engelmann & Carnine's *Theory of Instruction* (juxtaposition principles, "sameness"/"difference" example sets) was not retrievable; the example/non-example logic is cited from Stein & Rolf and Athabasca.

## KQ7. Cross-cutting: which techniques recur, and where primary and secondary differ

### Takeaway
Six moves recur across award citations *and* system studies: relationship/trust first; small steps with an exercise after each; many questions with every-student response; precise language in full sentences; worked models with concrete and abstract side by side; a summary that names the generalisation. The primary/secondary split in the sources is consistent: younger = play, affection, routine, choral repetition, fast response rate, stories and objects; older = relevance to identity and the wider world, autonomy in problem-solving and discussion, abstraction and formal terms, humour and authenticity rather than warmth-as-affection.

### Cited Findings (recurrence across sources)
- Relationship/trust before content: Zafirakou "Build the relationship, build that trust" [S8]; Sister Zeph "another mother" [S12]; Peterson "connections and providing individual support" [S19]; Smith "words of encouragement" [S21]; Coe "classroom climate... recognising students' self-worth" (moderate evidence) [S28].
- Small steps + practice after each: Rosenshine 2 [S28]; Shanghai "multiple small steps", "5 minutes or so at a time" [S27][S26]; DI "gradual skill introduction" [S42].
- Many questions, every student answers: Rosenshine 3 [S28]; Shanghai "every question planned... follow-up questions... also planned" [S24]; DI unison responses [S41]; Lemov Cold Call / Delivery Moves [S47][S46].
- Precise language, full sentences, stem sentences: Shanghai "addend + addend = sum", "China is the whole and Shanghai is a part" [S24]; NCETM secondary "precise mathematical language" [S26]; DfE "emphasis on correct mathematical language" [S27].
- Models/worked examples with concrete and abstract together: Rosenshine 4 [S28]; NCETM Representation and Structure [S25]; Shanghai Y2 lesson "the concrete and the abstract to be represented together" [S24]; DI examples/non-examples [S44].
- Struggle then compare then summarise: Japan hatsumon → neriage → matome [S37][S38][S39]; TIMSS 44% inventing [S35]; Shanghai lessons "commencing from a problem rather than lesson objectives" [S27].
- Summary/generalisation named at the end: matome [S39]; Shanghai "Construction of 'rules' (generalities) with whole class repetition" [S24]; NCETM secondary "results generalised algebraically" [S26].
- Daily review and retrieval: Rosenshine 1 and 10 [S28]; DI 90% review [S43]; Lemov Retrieval Practice / Knowledge Organisers [S46].
- Same-day intervention / no one left behind: Shanghai "same day intervention" [S24]; DfE "daily intervention" [S27]; NIFDI "taught at a faster rate... to catch up" [S42].

### Cited Findings (age differences in the sources)
- **Ages 3-8 (preschool/lower primary):** play to defuse violence and tension (Al Hroub) [S6]; social-emotional learning "at all ages" but foregrounded in preschool (Rosproy) [S16]; DI's K-3 evidence base with unison responses at 10-14/min and semicircle small groups [S41][S40]; Shanghai Y2 story-picture → words → number sentence, roles of each number, "At First / Middle / Now" [S24]; Disale's QR-linked audio poems and stories for primary readers [S10].
- **Ages 9-11 (upper primary):** Japanese grade-5 "most crowded cabin" lesson with four anticipated methods and neriage [S38]; Shanghai Y4 stem sentence across contexts; "deeper task" for fast finishers [S24]; Singapore P6 in-class dialogue sessions and problem posing, maths trail, times-table game [S50]; Atwell's 40 books/year with choice for middle grades [S5].
- **Ages 12-15 (lower secondary):** TIMSS grade 8: problem first, 53% of lessons with proofs, concept development in >3/4 of lessons [S35]; Shanghai secondary: 5-minute input bursts, no content differentiation, "articulate how they worked out", formal terms [S26]; Zafirakou: home-language greeting, art as language-free entry, applied curriculum [S8]; Tabichi: clubs and competitions to hold attendance [S9]; Yeo: drama and story-telling in English [S50].
- **Ages 16-18 (upper secondary):** Thorpe, Russell, Smith, Crosson: self-designed or redesigned courses tied to students' identity, civic action and "worldview" [S11][S18][S21][S20]; Peterson: daily noticing of "one good thing", accessibility of algebra-to-calculus [S19]; Manning: map-making projects to process trauma [S14]; MacDonnell: life-skills and fitness for 13-18 [S7].

### Inferences
- Warmth is documented as *affection and safety* for 6-8, as *fair challenge and belonging* for 12-18 ("constantly demanding more, but still recognising students' self-worth", S28). Humour is not documented as a technique in any citation here; it appears only as a personality descriptor (Shanghai teachers' "good humour", S24) — treat "use humour" as an inference, not a documented move.
- Pace: documented fast response cycles for the youngest (DI) and the longest uninterrupted thinking (~15 min) for grade 8 in Japan (S36); a monotone pace across bands is unsupported.
- Autonomy: documented as choice of reading (Atwell, ~11-14), own solution methods (Japan, 10-14), and course/project ownership (US NTOY, 16-18); not documented for 6-8 beyond play.

### Gaps
- No source directly compares the same award-winning teacher across age bands; the split above is assembled from different teachers and systems.
- Humour, teacher self-disclosure and pacing in seconds are not measured in any fetched source.

## Operational rules for an AI teacher (one Chinese K-12 student, live video + text board)

Each rule is one line, testable, with source id; a Chinese-classroom equivalent is given where natural. Rules are grouped by lesson stage, then by age band.

### A. Lesson opening (first 1-2 minutes)
1. Open with a 3-5 item retrieval quiz on the previous lesson before any new content (Rosenshine 1; TLAC Retrieval Practice) — S28, S46. CN: 复习导入.
2. State the single idea of the lesson in one board line and keep every section on that idea (Takahashi "single problem, single objective"; Shanghai "one key mathematical idea") — S37, S26. CN: 一课一得.
3. For ages 6-8, begin with a picture-story or object before any symbol (Shanghai Y2 car story; Al Hroub play) — S24, S6. CN: 情境导入.
4. For ages 12-18, begin from a problem or real issue, not from a list of objectives (DfE "commencing from a problem rather than lesson objectives"; Al Mansour project framing) — S27, S3.
5. Greet by name and, for a first lesson, with a phrase from the student's home dialect or interest (Zafirakou) — S8.

### B. Explanation and board use
6. Pre-write the exemplar answer to each section's key question before speaking (TLAC Exemplar Planning) — S46. CN: 备课教案.
7. Pair the concrete and the abstract on the same board line (picture/story next to the number sentence; Shanghai; NCETM Representation) — S24, S25. CN: 数形结合.
8. Never clear the board mid-lesson; add left-to-right so the finished board is the record of the lesson (bansho) — S39, S37. CN: 板书设计, 板书不擦.
9. Board layout per lesson: top-left problem, middle = student's own attempt verbatim, right = summary/rule — S39, S38.
10. Present new material in steps of about 5 minutes of input, each followed by student action (Shanghai secondary "5 minutes or so at a time"; Rosenshine 2) — S26, S28. CN: 讲练结合.
11. Teach a new procedure with a scripted example set and at least one non-example so only one interpretation survives (DI faultless communication) — S44, S41. CN: 正例反例.
12. Introduce the formal term the first time and insist on it thereafter ("product", "denominator"; "addend + addend = sum") — S26, S24. CN: 规范数学语言.
13. Ages 6-8: use role words for each number ("At first / Middle / Now") and ask what each number represents — S24.
14. Ages 9-15: after the student's method, put one or two anticipated alternative methods on the board and compare same/different, then advantages/limitations (neriage for a class of one) — S38.
15. Ages 16-18: generalise the result algebraically or as a principle on the board before closing the section (NCETM secondary) — S26. CN: 归纳总结.

### C. Questioning and checking
16. Ask a question every 30-60 seconds of explanation and require an answer from the student every time (Rosenshine 3; Shanghai every question planned) — S28, S24.
17. Decide and announce the answer mode before asking: say it, write it on the board, or work it silently (TLAC Delivery Moves; Silent Solo) — S46.
18. After asking, wait at least 3-5 seconds in silence before helping (TLAC Wait Time; Hiebert on letting confusion stand) — S47, S36. CN: 提问后等待.
19. Never accept "I don't know" as final: give a cue or a simpler sub-question, then return to the original question (TLAC No Opt Out) — S47.
20. Plan the follow-up question for the most likely wrong first answer before the lesson (Shanghai "follow-up questions... also planned") — S24.
21. Ask "why" and "how did you work it out" after correct answers, not only after wrong ones (NCETM secondary "deep questioning") — S26.
22. Ages 6-8: run fact drills as rapid call-and-response with a signal word, aiming for roughly 10+ responses per minute, and correct immediately (DI) — S41. CN: 抢答/齐答.
23. Ages 12-18: ask for a full-sentence explanation of the method before the answer is confirmed — S26, S24.

### D. Practice and the per-section exercise
24. Vary one element and hold the rest constant in each exercise so the exercise teaches the concept, not just repeats it (NCETM variation; Shanghai unknown in first/middle/now position) — S25, S24. CN: 变式练习.
25. Reuse the same stem sentence across at least three contexts (length, area, quantity) within the lesson ("The whole is..., a part is...") — S24. CN: 句式训练.
26. Keep the success rate high: if the student misses two items in a row, drop back a step before continuing (Rosenshine 7-8) — S28.
27. If the student finishes fast and correctly, give a deeper task on the same idea, not the next topic ("deepening, not acceleration", 动脑筋) — S24. CN: 培优.
28. Make 80-90% of each lesson's exercise items review of earlier material and 10-20% new (DI 90/10) — S43.
29. Ages 9-14: let the student attempt the section problem before any demonstration when they have the prerequisites; cap the attempt at ~2-3 minutes in a one-to-one setting, then compare methods (structured problem solving) — S37, S35, S39.
30. Ages 6-8 and for new procedures at any age: demonstrate first, then guided, then independent (DI I/We/You; Coe on discovery) — S43, S28.
31. Ages 11-14 literacy: give a choice of reading and set a volume target, then run short mini-lessons and a conference on the student's own text (Atwell) — S5.

### E. Feedback and correction
32. Correct errors immediately and specifically, by walking the student's own wrong story onto the board step by step until the contradiction shows (Shanghai 4 − 4 + 4) — S24. CN: 错题分析.
33. Praise the specific move or effort, never the easy success ("Praise for successful performance on an easy task... low perception") — S28.
34. Attribute success to effort and method, not ability, in every praise line — S28.
35. Do not open with confidence-building preambles; get one success first (Coe: "Start getting them to succeed") — S28.
36. During the student's work time, note which method they used and plan the comparison from it (kikan-shido seating-chart habit) — S38.
37. Withhold "right/wrong" while a method is being presented; judge after comparison (neriage) — S38.
38. Ages 12-18: correct behaviour or off-task moments privately and briefly, framed around purpose not compliance (TLAC Private Individual Correction) — S46.

### F. Section and lesson close
39. End each section with a one-line rule the student says back or writes (matome; Shanghai rule repetition) — S39, S24. CN: 课堂小结, 齐读结论.
40. End the lesson with an exit ticket of 2-3 items on today's idea and log the result for tomorrow's opening quiz (TLAC Exit Ticket; Rosenshine 10) — S47, S28.
41. If the exit ticket shows a gap, schedule a same-day or next-lesson-start micro-intervention on exactly that step (Shanghai same-day intervention) — S24, S27. CN: 补差.
42. Ages 6-8: close with a home-look task ("find parts and wholes at home") rather than a worksheet — S24.

### G. Rapport and climate by age band
43. Ages 6-8: name safety and affection explicitly, keep a fixed routine, use play/game framing for fluency work (Sister Zeph; Al Hroub; Parameswary game) — S12, S6, S50.
44. Ages 9-11: hold a short "dialogue session" about the student's own problem-posing or interests once per lesson (Yip Jee Cheng) — S50.
45. Ages 12-15: anchor the section in the student's world or identity and let them choose the example context (Zafirakou; Manning) — S8, S14.
46. Ages 16-18: treat the student as a problem-solver with a voice: ask for their position, connect to civic/real issues, be authentic rather than performatively warm (Crosson; Smith; Peterson) — S20, S21, S19.
47. Every band: keep expectations high while affirming worth ("constantly demanding more, but still recognising students' self-worth") — S28.
48. Every band: note one specific good thing the student did and say it at the close (Peterson "One Good Thing") — S19.
49. Never claim a learning style; never say "you are a visual learner" — S28.
50. Keep routine language (greeting, board headers, exercise hand-off) fixed and scripted so live attention goes to the student's answers (expert automaticity) — S30, S31.
