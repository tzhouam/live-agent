# International "how to teach" texts: operational techniques for a one-to-one AI teacher

Research notes, written 2026-09-30. Scope: what the best-known international evidence-based teaching texts *prescribe* at the level of teacher moves, lesson sequences and rules, and what the evidence behind them says. The consumer is an AI teacher persona that teaches one Chinese K-12 student at a time by live video with a text board beside it (lessons of 3-5 sections; each section = board points + short spoken lines + one graded exercise).

Conventions used throughout:
- **[Prescription]** = what a text tells teachers to do. **[Evidence]** = what a study or synthesis reports. **[Inference]** = my own translation to the agent setting.
- Every number is labelled *cited* (with its source), *estimate*, or *proposed target*. Nothing here is measured locally.
- Source ids (S1...S31) are defined in the source register below. "Opened" means the full text was fetched and read on 2026-09-30; "not opened" means only a snippet or a secondary summary was available, and claims from it are marked *not verified*.
- Tool constraints hit during this research: the WebSearch budget for the session was exhausted before the end (no further searches possible), and several hosts were unreachable from this network (deansforimpact.org refused connections; educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk, wiley.com, teacherhead.com, sec-ed.co.uk, researchschool.org.uk and oreilly.com returned 403; ibe.unesco.org and iaoed.org copies of the Rosenshine booklet returned 404 / certificate errors; web.archive.org is blocked for this tool; aft.org and springer.com serve a JavaScript wall). Those gaps are recorded per section.

## Rosenshine, "Principles of Instruction" (2010 IAE booklet; 2012 American Educator)

### Takeaway
Rosenshine prescribes an explicit-instruction cycle (daily review → small steps with practice after each → many questions to all students → models and worked examples → guided practice → checks for understanding → ~80 % success rate → scaffolds → monitored independent practice → weekly/monthly review) and backs each with classroom-observation and cognitive-science findings; the numbers he cites (5-8 min review, 23 vs 11 min of presentation, 82 % vs 73 % success, ≤30 s desk contacts, weekly/monthly review) are directly usable as agent parameters.

### Cited Findings
- **[Evidence]** The principles come from three sources: cognitive-science research on working-memory limits, observation studies of "master teachers" whose classes made the highest achievement-test gains, and research on cognitive supports (thinking aloud, scaffolds, models); Rosenshine stresses that the three sources "supplement and complement each other" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** The ten principles verbatim: (1) begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning; (2) present new material in small steps with student practice after each step; (3) ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students; (4) provide models; (5) guide student practice; (6) check for student understanding; (7) obtain a high success rate; (8) provide scaffolds for difficult tasks; (9) require and monitor independent practice; (10) engage students in weekly and monthly review — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** The 17-principle box (more detailed list from the same research): short review; small steps with practice; limit the amount of material at one time; clear and detailed instructions and explanations; many questions and check for understanding; high level of active practice for all students; guide students as they begin to practice; think aloud and model steps; provide models of worked-out problems; ask students to explain what they have learned; check the responses of all students; provide systematic feedback and corrections; use more time to provide explanations; provide many examples; reteach when necessary; prepare students for independent practice; monitor students when they begin independent practice — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** Master teachers "always did the experiential activities after, not before, the basic material was learned" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P1 [Evidence, cited]** In an elementary-mathematics experiment, teachers trained to spend eight minutes every day on review (checking homework, going over error problems, practising skills that need to become automatic) had higher-achieving classes; the most effective observed teachers began lessons with a five- to eight-minute review — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P1 [Prescription]** Daily review should: correct homework; review concepts/skills practised in homework; ask students where they had difficulties or made errors; review material where errors were made; review material that needs overlearning ("newly acquired skills should be practiced well beyond the point of initial mastery, leading to automaticity"); and recall the vocabulary/concepts needed for today's lesson because working memory is limited — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P2 [Evidence, cited]** Working memory "can only handle a few bits of information at once"; the most effective mathematics teachers spent about 23 minutes of a 40-minute period in lecture, demonstration, questioning and worked examples versus 11 minutes for the least effective; the least effective teachers asked only nine questions in a 40-minute period, then handed out worksheets and had to re-explain student by student — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P2 [Prescription]** Present only small amounts of new material at a time; master each point before the next; check understanding on each point and reteach when necessary; use "a series of short presentations using many examples"; worked example of small steps: teaching paragraph summarising as topic → main idea → supporting details, with modelling, thinking aloud and supervised practice at each step — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P3 [Evidence, cited]** The most successful teachers spent more than half of class time lecturing, demonstrating and asking questions; they also ask students "to explain the process they used to answer the question"; less successful teachers ask fewer questions and "almost no process questions"; teachers trained to follow new material with many factual and process questions (Good & Grouws 1979) had higher-scoring students — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P3 [Prescription]** Techniques for all-student responses: tell the answer to a neighbour; write a one- or two-sentence summary and share it; write the answer on a card and hold it up; raise hands if you know / if you agree with another answer; choral responses that must start together on a signal (otherwise only the fast students answer) — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P3 [Prescription]** Question stems (from King 1994) usable in literature, social science and science: "How are ___ and ___ alike?"; "What is the main idea of ___?"; "What are the strengths and weaknesses of ___?"; "In what way is ___ related to ___?"; "Compare ___ and ___ with regard to ___."; "What do you think causes ___?"; "How does ___ tie in with what we have learned before?"; "Which one is the best ___, and why?"; "What are some possible solutions for the problem of ___?"; "Do you agree or disagree with this statement: ___?"; "What do you still not understand about ___?" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P4 [Evidence]** Modelling with thinking aloud and worked examples (every step laid out) "reduce the cognitive load on their working memory"; used successfully in mathematics, science, writing and reading comprehension — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P4 [Prescription]** Sequence "providing a prompt, modeling, guiding practice, and supervising independent practice"; worked examples begin with the teacher modelling and explaining the steps *and* "the underlying principles for these steps"; Australian research alternated worked examples with problems during independent practice (study example → solve problem → study example → solve) and used partially completed problems so students actually attend to the example — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P5 [Evidence]** Students need time "rephrasing, elaborating, and summarizing new material" to store it; when rehearsal is too short they cannot store or use it; "it is also important that all students process the new material and receive feedback, so they do not inadvertently store partial information or a misconception" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P5 [Prescription]** Guidance "often consisted of the teacher working the first problems at the blackboard and explaining the reason for each step", then students working at the board and discussing procedures; the most successful teachers spent more time in guided practice, questioning, checking and correcting errors; classes with sufficient guided practice were more engaged during seatwork — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P6 [Prescription]** Check understanding by asking students to summarise the presentation so far, repeat directions or procedures, say whether they agree or disagree with another answer, think aloud while solving, or explain/defend a position; do **not** rely on "Are there any questions?" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P6 [Evidence]** Learners construct a "gist"; when background knowledge is weak they make errors that "are not errors so much as attempts by the students to be logical"; small steps + guided practice + checks limit misconceptions — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P7 [Evidence, cited]** Fourth-grade mathematics: 82 % of answers correct in the most successful teachers' classes vs 73 % for the least successful; "the optimal success rate for fostering student achievement appears to be about 80 percent", which shows learning *and* challenge — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P7 [Prescription]** "Practice can be a disaster if students are practicing errors"; a teacher who found students struggling during independent practice stopped the work, cancelled the homework and retaught next day; mastery learning (short units, master before proceeding, tutoring) is endorsed — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P8 [Prescription]** Scaffolds are temporary supports gradually withdrawn: teacher modelling and thinking aloud (labelling mental processes), cue cards, checklists, a model of the completed task to compare against, expert models to compare with the student's own; effective teachers "anticipate students' errors and warn them"; a four-step organising prompt (central box with title → 4-6 main ideas → boxes → 2-4 details each); checklist items such as "Have I found the most important information that tells me more about the main idea?" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P9 [Prescription/Evidence]** Independent practice "should involve the same material as the guided practice" (a slight variation is acceptable; a jump such as writing a paragraph after practising sentence types is not); overlearning to automaticity frees working memory; students were more engaged when the teacher circulated, with "optimal time for these contacts ... 30 seconds or less"; classes needing long desk explanations were the ones whose guided practice had been insufficient — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **P10 [Prescription/Evidence]** Review the previous week's work every Monday and the previous month's work every fourth Monday, with tests after the reviews; "even at the secondary level, classes that had weekly quizzes scored better on final exams than did classes with only one or two quizzes during the term"; "the best way to become an expert is through practice—thousands of hours of practice" — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **[Evidence]** The Sutton Trust review characterises Rosenshine's principles as a summary of "at least 40 years of research" whose evidence base starts with correlational observation studies and adds, "for each of these principles", experimental evidence that training teachers in the behaviour improves outcomes — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 3-5-section lesson: section 1 = daily review (target 5-8 min of a 40-min lesson, *cited* S1); sections 2-4 = one small step each (present → model/worked example → guided practice with questions → check → short independent exercise); last section = check + preview of the review that will return next week (P10).
- With one student, "check the responses of all students" collapses to "get a response from the student to every check" — never accept silence or "any questions?".
- The 80 % success target (*cited*) is the natural control variable for the agent's difficulty selection: if graded exercises fall well below ~80 %, insert more worked examples and guided steps; if well above, advance.

### Gaps
- The 2010 IAE booklet (S2) could not be opened; the 2012 article is an adaptation and reproduces its principles, but any wording that differs in the booklet is not verified.
- Rosenshine gives no explicit rules by age band beyond noting that many successful review programmes were "especially in the elementary grades" and that weekly quizzes help "even at the secondary level".

## Doug Lemov, Teach Like a Champion (1.0 / 2.0 / 3.0)

### Takeaway
Lemov's named techniques are the most granular "teacher moves" available; the official site and field-guide excerpt supply verifiable definitions for No Opt Out (with its four formats), Cold Call, Stretch It, Show Call, Show Me, Exit Ticket, Do Now, Positive Framing, Precise Praise, Wait Time, Retrieval Practice, Circulate, Everybody Writes, Ratio, Turn and Talk and Habits of Discussion; the rest (Right Is Right sub-parts, Format Matters, Name the Steps, Board = Paper, At Bats, Warm/Strict, Own and Track, Habits of Attention) could not be verified online and must be taken from the book.

### Cited Findings
- **[Edition]** *Teach Like a Champion 3.0* (Jossey-Bass, 2021) contains 63 numbered techniques across eleven chapters, adds an introductory chapter on mental models, a new chapter on Lesson Preparation, and 10 new techniques; the techniques were compiled from footage of teachers "whose classes outperformed expectations" — [S4](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques). (ISBN 9781119712619 per the Wiley catalogue entry in search results; *not verified* against the publisher page, which returned 403.)
- **No Opt Out [Prescription]** "When a student begins by being unable or unwilling to answer, you should strive to make the sequence end as often as possible with the student giving a right or valid answer." Four formats: (1) you provide the answer, the student repeats it; (2) another student provides the answer, the initial student repeats it; (3) you provide a cue, the student uses it to find the answer; (4) another student provides a cue, the initial student uses it. "An incorrect answer is not opting out. But saying 'I don't know' in good faith or with impassivity is opting out." Raise rigour by "wrapping up the sequence with a request for another correct answer or an explanation of the 'why.'" — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf)
- **No Opt Out [Prescription, example language]** "Take a little more time to speak your thoughts. I'll wait." / "Give it some more thought. I'll be back." / "We can work on this problem together. I'll bring it back to you in a moment." The "However/Best" rule: a student may say "I don't know" only if followed by "however" and a best try ("I don't know; however, I think I need to multiply here"). Start with questions that "have one clear, correct answer and can be answered in a single sentence". The best cue "calls attention to a resource that a student has overlooked", e.g. where the answer can be found or "the first thing he should do" — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf)
- **Format Matters [Prescription]** The field guide says Format Matters "suggests several criteria you can request the student to follow before you conclude he is opting out, including audibility and complete sentence format", e.g. "Say it as a complete sentence. The question I'm asking is..." — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf)
- **Right Is Right [Prescription]** Students may "opt out" by answering "some question other than the one you asked"; the response is "Delia, I'd like you to focus on the question I've asked. My question right now is..." — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf); the official blog frames it as "Right is Right Starts With The Way You Ask The Question" — [S3b-right](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/right-is-right/)
- **Cold Call [Prescription]** "Calling on students regardless of whether they raise their hands to increase rigor and participation" — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/); "the teacher names who answers rather than taking hands" — [S4](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques); "No Opt Out is especially useful in supporting Cold Call" — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf)
- **Stretch It [Prescription]** After a student gets it right, "ask him another question to let him apply the idea, show how much he can do, maybe even shine a little"; combined with No Opt Out the teacher asks "a series of similar questions that let him practice the key skill and show others (and himself) that he can master the skill" (example: Sarah Salazar, grade 10-11 maths) — [S3b-stretch](https://teachlikeachampion.org/blog/tlac-2-0-adding-stretch-it-to-no-opt-out/)
- **Show Call [Prescription]** The teacher takes a student's written work (without asking permission, to build accountability) and projects it with a document camera for class analysis; ask "What did they do here?" so peers identify the strategy; choose exemplars showing different approaches; it "builds a really positive culture where students take pride in their work" — [S3b-showcall](https://teachlikeachampion.org/blog/katie-mcnickles-show-call/)
- **Show Me [Prescription]** Checking understanding while "gather[ing] data on student mastery" (e.g. all students display answers) — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/)
- **Exit Ticket [Prescription]** An assessment giving "class-wide feedback on lesson comprehension" — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/); "a short written check in the last minutes of a lesson" — [S4](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)
- **Do Now [Prescription]** An opening activity that is ready when students arrive; the official site pairs it with a Retrieval Practice guide "using Do Nows" — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/)
- **Retrieval Practice (3.0) [Prescription/Evidence]** "the act of recalling previously encountered information into working memory, or conscious thinking"; "brief spurts of Retrieval Practice help students solidify information in their long-term memories"; "Understanding is not learning until it is encoded in long-term memory" — [S3b-retrieval](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/retrieval-practice/)
- **Wait Time [Prescription]** Allowing "time to think before answering" for deeper responses; the site offers "100 Phrases for Narrating During Wait Time" — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/); "pausing after a question rather than filling the silence" — [S4](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)
- **Everybody Writes / Ratio [Prescription]** Writing "can cause every student to answer a question instead of just a few who volunteer to speak" and creates "desirable difficulty"; Ratio = the proportion of student participation/thinking versus teacher talk — [S3b-writes](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/everybody-writes/), [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/)
- **Turn and Talk / Habits of Discussion [Prescription]** Turn and Talk is "a short structured paired discussion"; Habits of Discussion teaches conventions "including audibility and agreement markers" — [S4](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)
- **Positive Framing [Prescription]** Framing corrections "to reinforce faith and trust" through positive language (site offers an examples/non-examples sheet) — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/)
- **Precise Praise (technique #59 in 3.0) [Prescription]** Name "specific actions a student is taking" so they can be replicated; distinguish acknowledgement (recognising completion) from praise (celebrating effort or strategy); praise effort and engagement with challenge rather than ability (citing Dweck); keep praise genuine and specific; "PIPP" = Private Individual Precise Praise delivered one-to-one — [S3b-praise](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/precise-praise/)
- **Warm/Strict (#60) and Firm, Calm, Finesse (#54) [Prescription]** Named on the official site as part of "building positive classroom culture" (kindergarten example) but with no definition reproduced online — [S3b-warmstrict](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/warm-strict/)
- **Circulate [Prescription]** Moving throughout the classroom using "affirmation and accountability" tools — [S3b-resources](https://teachlikeachampion.org/resources/grab-and-go/)
- **Culture of Error / Check for Understanding / Active Observation [Prescription]** Culture of Error "includes but is also broader than establishing psychological safety"; Check for Understanding = "gathering data" and "perceiving and responding to errors"; Active Observation = "building systems to harvest data and observations about student thinking" — [S3b-error](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/culture-of-error/), [S3b-cfu](https://teachlikeachampion.org/tag/check-for-understanding/)

### Inferences
- One-to-one translation: Cold Call → the agent always addresses the student by name and asks for a specific answer; No Opt Out → after a wrong/blank answer the agent gives a cue (format 3), then returns to the *same* question until the student states the right answer, then Stretch It with a near-transfer question; Everybody Writes → the student types/writes before the agent reacts; Show Call → the agent puts the student's submitted answer on the board and asks "What did you do here?"; Exit Ticket → the graded exercise in the final section; Do Now → the graded retrieval exercise in section 1.
- Format Matters ("complete sentence", "audible") maps to a rule that the agent requests a full-sentence or fully written answer before grading.

## Lesson-design frameworks: Hunter, Gagné, Understanding by Design, revised Bloom

### Takeaway
Hunter's elements (anticipatory set, objective/purpose, input, modeling, checking for understanding, guided practice, independent practice, closure) and Gagné's nine events give near-identical lesson skeletons that fit a 3-5-section lesson; UbD adds backward design (decide the evidence of understanding before the activities) and the warning that "understanding cannot simply be told"; Krathwohl's revision of Bloom supplies a verb ladder (remember → understand → apply → analyze → evaluate → create) for grading question difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- **Hunter [Prescription]** Hunter & Russell (1976) listed seven elements "that should be considered when designing a lesson": anticipatory set, objective, input, modeling, checking for understanding, guided practice, independent practice; they "do not list them as steps, nor do they indicate that the elements are to be carried out in order"; Wolfe: "Those seven instructional elements are not a recipe to be followed step by step in every lesson"; a teacher may withhold the objective or postpone practice when it serves learning; "teaching is decision making" — [S5b](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/journals/ed_lead/el_198702_wolfe.pdf)
- **Hunter [Prescription, definitions]** Anticipatory set = "a short activity, dispatch or prompt that focuses the students' attention and ties previous lessons to today's lesson"; Purpose = "an explanation of the importance of this lesson and a statement concerning what students will be able to do"; Input = "the vocabulary, skills, and concepts to be learned"; Modeling = "the teacher demonstrates what is to be learned"; Checking for understanding = "a variety of questioning strategies to determine if the students are understanding"; Guided practice = "the teacher leads the students through the steps necessary to perform the skill"; Independent practice = "the teacher releases students to practice on their own"; Closure = "a review or wrap-up of the lesson" — [S5a](https://csun.edu/science/ref/plans/lesson_design_hunter.html)
- **Gagné [Prescription]** Nine events (Gagné, Briggs & Wager 1992): 1 gain attention (novelty, uncertainty, surprise; thought-provoking questions); 2 inform learners of objectives (describe required performance and criteria); 3 stimulate recall of prior learning (ask about prior concepts; relate previous content); 4 present the content (organised, meaningful grouping; explanations after demonstrations; varied media); 5 provide learning guidance ("help students learn how to learn": scaffolding that reduces as competence grows, examples and non-examples, analogies, metaphors, visual images); 6 elicit performance (practice, formative tasks); 7 provide feedback ("timely feedback ... to allow students to identify gaps in understanding before it is too late"; types: confirmatory, evaluative, remedial (directs toward the answer without revealing it), descriptive/analytic); 8 assess performance (pre/post tests, rubrics); 9 enhance retention and transfer (connect to prior/future learning, re-use earlier test questions, convert information between formats such as concept maps) — [S6](https://www.niu.edu/citl/resources/guides/instructional-guide/gagnes-nine-events-of-instruction.shtml)
- **UbD [Prescription]** Seven tenets include: focus on understanding and transfer; "understanding is revealed when students autonomously make sense of and transfer their learning through authentic performance"; plan backward through three stages (Desired Results → Evidence → Learning Plan); "teachers are coaches of understanding, not mere purveyors of content"; review units against design standards; continual improvement — [S7](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf)
- **UbD [Prescription]** Stage 1 asks "What should students know, understand, and be able to do? What is the ultimate transfer we seek ... What essential questions will be explored in-depth?"; sample essential questions: "How does where we live influence how we live?", "What's the best way of showing (or representing) ___? In what other way(s) can this be represented?"; "acquisition of content is a means, in the service of meaning making and transfer" — [S7](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf)
- **UbD [Prescription]** Stage 2: "think like assessors before designing specific units and lessons"; six facets of understanding — explain (in own words, teach it to others, justify, show reasoning), interpret (images, analogies, stories, models), apply (new and complex contexts), perspective, empathy, self-knowledge; "In mathematics, application, interpretation, and explanation are the most natural"; performance tasks are culminating, "not intended for use in daily lessons" — [S7](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf)
- **UbD [Prescription]** Stage 3: code learning events as T (transfer), M (meaning making) or A (acquisition) so all three are addressed; "Understanding cannot simply be told; the learner has to actively construct meaning (or misconceptions and forgetfulness will ensue)"; transfer requires applying learning to new situations "and receive timely feedback" — [S7](https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf)
- **Revised Bloom [Prescription, definitions]** Cognitive process dimension (Krathwohl 2002, Table 3): 1 Remember — "retrieving relevant knowledge from long-term memory" (1.1 recognizing, 1.2 recalling); 2 Understand — "determining the meaning of instructional messages" (interpreting, exemplifying, classifying, summarizing, inferring, comparing, explaining); 3 Apply — "carrying out or using a procedure in a given situation" (executing, implementing); 4 Analyze — "breaking material into its constituent parts and detecting how the parts relate" (differentiating, organizing, attributing); 5 Evaluate — "making judgments based on criteria and standards" (checking, critiquing); 6 Create — "putting elements together to form a novel, coherent whole" (generating, planning, producing). Knowledge dimension: A Factual, B Conceptual, C Procedural, D Metacognitive ("knowledge of cognition in general as well as awareness and knowledge of one's own cognition") — [S8](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)
- **Revised Bloom [Evidence]** Krathwohl notes the hierarchy is not strict: some Understand processes (e.g. explaining) are more complex than some Apply processes (e.g. executing), though the category "center points" still form a simple-to-complex scale — [S8](https://rvc-web-app01.live01.azure.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/About/Academic%20Quality,%20Regulations%20and%20Procedures/Academic%20Quality%20Assurance%20and%20Enhancement%20Procedures/Module%20Development%20and%20Approval/A%20Revision%20of%20Bloom's%20Taxonomy%20-%20An%20Overview.%20David%20R.%20Krathwohl.pdf)
- **Revised Bloom [Reference]** Anderson & Krathwohl (eds.) 2001, *A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing*, Longman — [S32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_taxonomy)

### Inferences
- A 4-section agent lesson maps cleanly: S1 = anticipatory set + objective + recall (Gagné 1-3, Hunter set/purpose, Rosenshine P1); S2 = input + modelling (Gagné 4-5, Rosenshine P2/P4); S3 = guided practice + checking (Gagné 6-7, Rosenshine P5/P6); S4 = independent exercise + closure (Gagné 8-9, Hunter closure, Lemov Exit Ticket).
- Backward design for the agent: write the final exercise (the evidence of understanding) *before* writing the explanation lines, then check every board point is needed to pass it.
- Grade each exercise's Bloom level; within a lesson, move exercises from Remember/Understand (S1) to Apply/Analyze (S3-S4); Create-level tasks are rare in a 40-minute one-to-one lesson (UbD says culminating tasks are not for daily lessons).

### Gaps
- The WHERETO learning-plan mnemonic was not found in the UbD white paper text and is not verified here.
- No opened source gives effect sizes or experimental evidence for Hunter's or Gagné's sequences as wholes; they are design frameworks, not tested interventions.

## Dylan Wiliam, Black & Wiliam: formative assessment

### Takeaway
Black & Wiliam claim formative-assessment innovations yield 0.4-0.7 SD gains (cited, from their 1998 review) and prescribe five strategies operationalised as concrete moves: shared success criteria with annotated exemplars, pre-planned diagnostic ("hinge"/"big") questions answered by every student within ~2 minutes and read in ~30 seconds, wait time of at least 3 seconds, comment-only feedback that leaves the learner something to do ("find them and fix them"), students writing their own test questions, self-assessment signals (traffic lights), and adjusting instruction within a day or two.

### Cited Findings
- **[Evidence, cited]** Formative-assessment innovations "typically produced 0.4-0.7 effect sizes"; "an effect size of 0.4 would mean that the average pupil involved in an innovation would record the same achievement as a pupil in the top 35%"; "improved formative assessment helps low achievers more than other students and so reduces the range of achievement while raising achievement overall" — [S9](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/) (original: *Phi Delta Kappan* 80(2), Oct 1998, 139-148)
- **[Prescription]** Questioning: when teachers "do not wait long enough to allow pupils to think out their answers", only recall questions survive; remedies are "giving pupils time to respond; asking them to discuss their thinking in pairs or in small groups; giving pupils a choice between different possible answers and asking them to vote"; dialogue should be "thoughtful, reflective, focused to evoke and explore understanding" — [S9](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/)
- **[Evidence/Prescription]** "If pupils are given only marks or grades, they do not benefit from the feedback"; feedback "should be about the particular qualities of his or her work, with advice on what he or she can do to improve, and should avoid comparisons with other pupils"; tests should give "specific guidance on strengths and weaknesses, preferably without any overall marks" — [S9](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/)
- **[Prescription]** "Self-assessment by pupils, far from being a luxury, is in fact an essential component"; pupils need the desired goal, evidence of present position, and "a way to close the gap"; "it is better to have frequent short tests than infrequent long ones"; "any new learning should first be tested within about a week of a first encounter"; "for formative purposes, a test at the end of a unit or teaching module is pointless; it is too late to work with the results" — [S9](https://kappanonline.org/inside-the-black-box-raising-standards-through-classroom-assessment/)
- **[Definition]** "Practice in a classroom is formative to the extent that evidence about student achievement is elicited, interpreted, and used by teachers, learners, or their peers, to make decisions about the next steps in instruction that are likely to be better, or better founded, than the decisions they would have taken in the absence of the evidence" — [S10](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/files/9119063/Black2009_Developing_the_theory_of_formative_assessment.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** Five key strategies (Wiliam & Thompson 2007 framework, crossing where the learner is going / is now / how to get there with teacher / peer / learner): (1) clarifying and sharing learning intentions and criteria for success; (2) engineering effective classroom discussions and other learning tasks that elicit evidence of student understanding; (3) providing feedback that moves learners forward; (4) activating students as instructional resources for one another; (5) activating students as the owners of their own learning — [S10](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/files/9119063/Black2009_Developing_the_theory_of_formative_assessment.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** Formative assessment is about "moments of contingency" — points where instruction can proceed in different directions depending on student responses; synchronous examples include "teachers' 'real-time' adjustments during one-on-one teaching"; asynchronous examples include 'exit passes' at the end of a lesson used to plan the next — [S10](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/files/9119063/Black2009_Developing_the_theory_of_formative_assessment.pdf)
- **Hinge questions [Prescription, cited numbers]** A hinge question is asked "somewhere in the middle of the lesson"; the teacher "gets a response from every single student"; students should answer within two minutes; the teacher must "collect and interpret all the student responses in 30 seconds or less"; the critical design criterion is that "students with the right thinking and students with the wrong thinking" give different answers (no right answer for the wrong reason); Wiliam gives no fixed percentage — proceed "if most students have understood well enough" — [S11](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/designing-great-hinge-questions)
- **Big/hinge question with contingency [Prescription]** Example: after teaching global warming, "What can we do to preserve the ozone layer?" with five options answered by every student (fingers or cards); "if every student has responded correctly, she moves on. If no-one gets it right, she might teach it again, preferably in a different way. But if there is a lot of variation ... she can direct the students to talk about their answers with their neighbours"; a thesis-statement example with eight options where H (announcing the essay topic) is a common confusion — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Closed diagnostic questions [Prescription]** "Is calculus exact or approximate?", "Would your mass be the same on the moon?" — closed questions are valuable when "each question is focused on a specific misconception"; treating all wrong answers as equivalent wastes the information; questions should be prepared "upstream", anticipating which misconception produces which wrong answer — [S13](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/IAEA%2004%20paper.pdf)
- **Short cycle [Prescription/Evidence]** "if you're not using information to make a difference to your teaching within a day or two then it's unlikely to make a difference to student achievement"; the short cycle "minute by minute, and day by day" matters most — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Comment-only marking [Prescription]** Instead of "15 out of 20", tell students "five of their answers were wrong, and that they should find them and fix them"; English teachers mark a G/S/P letter in the margin per grammar/spelling/punctuation error — "for the stronger students ... simply put a dot in the margin for each error, and for the weaker student ... indicate where in the line the error was"; "the feedback gives something to the learner to do so that the immediate reaction of the learner is that they have to think"; comment-only marking "is a good thing to do" but politically difficult where parents expect grades — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Butler's marks-vs-comments finding [Evidence]** Hattie & Timperley report Butler's (1988) study in which feedback through comments led to gains while feedback "by marks or giving praise did not" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **Three-quarters test [Prescription]** Give the unit test three-quarters of the way through so there is time to remediate before moving on — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc); four teachers in the KMOFAP project planned module tests "before the end of the module thus providing time for remediation" — [S13](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/IAEA%2004%20paper.pdf)
- **Sharing intentions [Prescription/Evidence]** Success-criteria lists "are often opaque to students"; give "annotated student work to 'flesh out' learning intentions"; in a 1994 study, students who made up test questions (with answers) scored highest compared with those who revised or did mock tests — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Self-assessment signals [Prescription]** Red/green discs (or cups) flipped by students during explanation; red triggers a peer explanation; traffic lights (red/amber/green, or smiley faces) used with follow-up of the cases signalling incomplete understanding; "One of these twenty answers is wrong: find it and fix it!" — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc), [S13](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/IAEA%2004%20paper.pdf)
- **Exit passes [Prescription]** Each student answers a key question before leaving, e.g. "Why can't a probability be greater than one?", and the teacher adjusts the next lesson — [S13](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/IAEA%2004%20paper.pdf)
- **Peer checking [Prescription]** "Pre-flight checklist": a peer signs off a checklist (diagram in pencil and labelled, title, margin) before work can be submitted, so the checker must internalise the success criteria — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Wait time [Prescription, cited]** "students learn more if teachers wait at least three seconds after asking a question before providing hints or moving on to another student", yet teachers do not do it; habit change comes from "never asking a question without waiting three seconds"; teachers should change only "two or three things in a year" — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Random selection by age [Prescription]** Lollipop sticks (random name draw) "works well with younger children, but may not work so well for older students", though Wiliam has seen it used effectively with A-level students because randomness removes "why are you picking on me?" — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **Teachers' own plans (KMOFAP) [Evidence]** Teachers' action plans most often targeted questioning ("more open questions, allowing students more time to think of answers or starting the lesson with a focal question"), comment-only marking (mentioned by nearly half), sharing objectives, and self-assessment via traffic lights — [S13](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/IAEA%2004%20paper.pdf)
- **Rowe's wait-time research [Evidence, cited via secondary]** Rowe (1986, *J. Teacher Education* 37(1), 43-50) observed teachers pausing "a second or less" after a question; once average wait time passed roughly 2.7 seconds, response length rose "by between 300% and 700% depending on the study", reasoned answers became more common, "I don't know" replies fell, and quieter learners answered unprompted; "wait time 2" (after a student's answer) is described as "a longer, more important one after the first answer"; the briefing grades the evidence "Developing" (narrative synthesis of dissertations and conference papers, no confidence intervals) — [S18](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/wait-time-three-seconds)

### Inferences
- For a single student, the "every student responds" requirement is automatically met; what carries over is the *design* rule: each section's exercise should be a hinge-style question where wrong answers map to specific misconceptions, so the agent's next move (advance / re-explain differently / probe) is decided by which wrong answer was given.
- The agent's grader should report "n of your answers are wrong — find and fix them" before revealing which, for students who can bear it (Wiliam scales the hint by strength: dot for stronger, location for weaker).
- Wait time for an AI teacher = do not interrupt or hint for at least 3 s (*cited* threshold) after a question, and hold a second pause after the student's first answer before evaluating.

### Gaps
- "Two stars and a wish", ABCD cards named as such, mini-whiteboards and "no hands up except to ask a question" appear in Wiliam's books and talks but were not found in the sources opened here (teacherhead, sec-ed and NWEA summaries returned 403/404); *not verified*.
- Butler's 1988 numbers (grades vs comments vs both) are only reported qualitatively via S15.
- The 0.4-0.7 range is a 1998 narrative-review figure; later re-analyses (not opened) are reported to give smaller average effects — flag as contested.

## John Hattie, Visible Learning: effect sizes and their critics

### Takeaway
Hattie's lists rank feedback (0.70), teacher clarity (0.75), classroom discussion (0.82), scaffolding (0.82), direct instruction (0.60), mastery learning (0.57), spaced practice (0.60), metacognitive strategies (0.60) and self-reported grades (1.33) well above his 0.40 "hinge point" while worked examples (0.37) and interleaving (0.21) score low in the 2017 list (all *cited* as reproduced by visible-learning.org), but statisticians (Bergeron & Rivard 2017) show the method averages incommensurable effect sizes, produced impossible negative "common language effect" probabilities and uses an arbitrary benchmark, so the numbers should guide priorities only weakly.

### Cited Findings
- **[Evidence, cited]** 2017 backup list of 256 influences, hinge point 0.40: feedback 0.70; teacher clarity 0.75; direct instruction 0.60; worked examples 0.37; spaced vs mass practice 0.60; classroom discussion 0.82; mastery learning 0.57; self-reported grades 1.33; Piagetian programs 1.28; metacognitive strategies 0.60; teacher-student relationships 0.52; scaffolding 0.82; questioning 0.48; deliberate practice 0.79; interleaved practice 0.21; rehearsal and memorization 0.73; elaboration and organization 0.75; summarization 0.79; transfer strategies 0.86; concept mapping 0.64; teacher credibility 0.90; collective teacher efficacy 1.57; cognitive task analysis 1.29; strategy to integrate with prior knowledge 0.93; effort 0.77; homework 0.29; class size 0.21; matching style of learning 0.31; inquiry-based teaching 0.40; problem-based learning 0.26; boredom -0.49; ability grouping 0.12; retention (holding back) -0.32 — [S14b](https://visible-learning.org/backup-hattie-ranking-256-effects-2017/); the same values appear on the 252-influence page, which adds "the story underlying the data has hardly changed over time even though some effect sizes were updated" — [S14a](https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/)
- **[Evidence, cited]** Hattie's 1999 synthesis (over 500 meta-analyses, 450,000 effect sizes): average effect of schooling 0.40 (SE 0.05); feedback averaged 0.79 across 12 meta-analyses (196 studies, 6,972 effects), "in the top 5 to 10 highest influences", alongside direct instruction 0.93, reciprocal teaching 0.86, prior cognitive ability 0.71; compare acceleration 0.47, socioeconomic 0.44, homework 0.41, calculators 0.24, reducing class size 0.12, retention -0.12 — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, note]** The feedback and direct-instruction values differ between the 1999 synthesis (0.79, 0.93) and the 2017 list (0.70, 0.60), so any single "Hattie number" must carry its list version — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf), [S14b](https://visible-learning.org/backup-hattie-ranking-256-effects-2017/)
- **[Critique]** Bergeron & Rivard: Hattie's "most blatant calculation error" is the common language effect (CLE), noticed by Topphol (2012), "flagrant to the point of giving negative probabilities or probabilities superior to 100%"; the method groups factors "without defining inclusion and exclusion criteria"; "Hattie computes averages that do not make any sense" (head in the oven, feet in the freezer); Cohen's d "simply cannot be used as a universal measure of impact"; conclusion: "we must therefore absolutely qualify Hattie's methodology as pseudoscience" and "no one should replicate this methodology" — [S16](https://mje.mcgill.ca/article/download/9475/7228/30586)
- **[Critique]** Bergeron & Rivard also note the sign of an effect depends on the arbitrary direction of comparison (class size), and that reducing everything "to one single number" loses dose, duration, population and context — [S16](https://mje.mcgill.ca/article/download/9475/7228/30586)
- **[Critique, secondary]** Didau summarises Simpson: the meta-meta-analysis "is like blending apples, oranges, and maybe a few wrenches" without accounting for study quality or design; Eacott (2018): the data "glosses over contradictions, offering a false sense of certainty"; Kamenetz (2015): the 0.4 hinge "falsely divides good from bad" — [S17](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/visible-learning-invisible-errors)
- **[Critique, search snippet only]** Simpson's argument that effect sizes depend on comparison-group inequality, range restriction and measurement design (a "category error" to rank interventions by them) and Slavin's "shoveling meta-analyses containing massive bias into meta-meta-analyses" appeared in search summaries but the primary papers were not opened — *not verified*.

### Inferences
- Use Hattie only as a coarse priority list that agrees with Rosenshine/Wiliam (feedback, clarity, scaffolding, discussion/questioning, spaced practice, mastery), and never as a quantitative dial; where Hattie disagrees with controlled cognitive-science findings (worked examples 0.37, interleaving 0.21) prefer the experimental literature (CLT, Make It Stick).
- "Self-reported grades" (1.33) is a student-expectation effect, not a technique; its operational analogue is asking the student to predict their score before an exercise, then comparing (calibration).

## Hattie & Timperley, "The Power of Feedback" (2007)

### Takeaway
Feedback should answer three questions (Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?) at the task, process or self-regulation level, move students from task toward process and regulation, and avoid the self level (praise), which the review finds least effective (praise effect sizes 0.12-0.14; "no praise has a greater impact on achievement than praise" when praise accompanies task feedback).

### Cited Findings
- **[Model]** Three feedback questions — "Where am I going? (What are the goals?), How am I going? (What progress is being made toward the goal?), and Where to next? (What activities need to be undertaken to make better progress?)" — correspond to "feed up, feed back, and feed forward" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Model]** Four levels: task (FT: "whether work is correct or incorrect", "You need to include more about the Treaty of Versailles"); process (FP: "You need to edit this piece of writing by attending to the descriptors you have used"); self-regulation (FR: "You already know the key features of the opening of an argument. Check to see whether you have incorporated them in your first paragraph"); self (FS: "You are a great student", "That's an intelligent response, well done"). "FS is the least effective, FR and FP are powerful in terms of deep processing and mastery of tasks, and FT is powerful when the task information subsequently is useful for improving strategy processing or enhancing self-regulation" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, cited]** About 90 % of teachers' questions are at the task/information level (Airasian 1997); teachers "commonly mix corrective feedback with information at the self level, which dilutes the power of the FT (e.g., 'Good boy, that is correct')"; corrective feedback alone shows 1.13 (Lysakowski & Walberg 1982), 0.82 (Walberg 1982), 0.74 (Tenenbaum & Goldring 1989) — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** "FT is more powerful when it is about faulty interpretations, not lack of information. If students lack necessary knowledge, further instruction is more powerful than feedback information"; task feedback often does not generalise to other tasks; "too much feedback within a level may even detract from performance" — over-specific FT led LOGO students to trial-and-error instead of strategies — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, cited]** Table 2 (Hattie's database): cues 1.10; feedback 0.95; reinforcement 0.94; video/audio feedback 0.64; corrective feedback 0.37; delayed vs immediate 0.34; reward 0.31; immediate vs delayed 0.24; punishment 0.20; praise 0.14; programmed instruction -0.04. "Programmed instruction, praise, punishment, and extrinsic rewards were the least effective" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, cited]** Kluger & DeNisi (1996) moderators: feedback designed to discourage the student -0.14 vs 0.33; praise about the task 0.09 vs no praise 0.34; feedback from a computer 0.41 vs 0.23; lots of feedback 0.32 vs little 0.39; very complex tasks 0.03 vs not complex 0.55; difficult goals 0.51 vs "do your best" 0.30; much threat to self-esteem 0.08 vs little 0.47 — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, cited]** Tangible rewards: Deci, Koestner & Ryan found a -0.34 correlation with task performance, -0.68 for interesting tasks (0.18 uninteresting), -0.78 when delivered in a controlling manner ("performed as they 'should'") — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, cited]** Praise: Wilkinson (1981) 0.12; Kluger & DeNisi (1998) 0.09, "and found that no praise has a greater impact on achievement than praise"; but distinguish praise "directed to the effort, self-regulation, engagement, or processes relating to the task" (can enhance self-efficacy) from praise about the person; students *do* like praise — 26 % of adolescents preferred loud public praise, 64 % quiet private praise (Sharp 1985); students preferred praise for trying hard rather than for ability — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, age]** "Older students perceived praise after success or neutral feedback after failure as an indication that the teacher perceived their ability to be low ... The same effects were not evident for younger students, however, who perceived praise after success as an indication of high ability and criticism after failure as a sign of low ability" (Meyer et al. 1979; Meyer 1982) — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence, timing, cited]** "Immediate error correction during task acquisition (FT) can result in faster rates of acquisition, whereas immediate error correction during fluency building can detract from the learning of automaticity"; Kulik & Kulik (1988): at task level (tests) some delay is beneficial (0.36) while at process level immediate feedback helps (0.28); Clariana et al. (2000): delayed-feedback effect sizes -0.06 for easy items, 0.35 midrange, 1.17 difficult — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence]** "Disconfirmation with corrective information can be effective, but disconfirmation without this information is of little use"; "errors and disconfirmation are most powerful in climates in which they are seen as leading to future learning" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Conclusion]** "Feedback at the self or personal level (usually praise), on the other hand, is rarely effective. Praise is rarely directed at addressing the three feedback questions"; effective feedback "moves from the task to the processes ... to regulation about continuing beyond the task to more challenging tasks and goals" — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Prescription, Marzano]** Feedback should be corrective, timely and specific to a criterion (search-snippet summary of Marzano, Pickering & Pollock; *not verified* in the opened slides, which cover only the first three strategies in detail) — [S27](https://media.cobbk12.org/media/WWWCobb/medialib/marzanos-nine-top-strategies.6990a445329.pdf)

### Inferences
- Grading template for the agent: (1) task: right/wrong and what is missing; (2) process: which step or strategy to check; (3) regulation: a prompt to self-check against the criteria; (4) no person-praise; if any praise, praise the strategy/effort in one clause and keep it separate from the correctness verdict.
- When the student lacks the prerequisite (not a misapplication), the agent should re-teach (worked example) rather than write longer feedback.
- Timing: give immediate correction during a new skill's guided practice; during fluency/independent practice let the student finish the set, then feed back; for hard items, a short delay (e.g. after the student's attempt at the next item) is acceptable.

### Gaps
- The paper's formal definition sentence of feedback was not captured verbatim (only its model), so it is paraphrased here.

## Cognitive science of learning: cognitive load theory, Willingham, Make It Stick, Deans for Impact, dual coding and Mayer

### Takeaway
The cognitive-science texts converge on: working memory is small, so present in small steps and use worked examples and faded guidance for novices (then switch to problem solving as expertise grows — expertise reversal); "memory is the residue of thought", so make the student think about meaning, use stories and concrete examples, and build factual knowledge before demanding skill; retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaboration and generation beat rereading; and for a talking video with a board, Mayer's principles say narration + graphics beats narration + duplicate text, keep labels next to figures, signal what matters, segment, pre-train key terms, use a conversational human voice, and do not expect the talking head itself to add learning.

### Cited Findings
- **CLT worked-example effect [Evidence]** "Students provided problems to solve learn less than students presented identical problems with worked examples"; worked examples reduce "unnecessary overload on their working memory" — [S25](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/cognitive-load-theory-teaching-strategies); Rosenshine describes the same research: alternate worked examples with problems and use partially completed problems — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **CLT expertise reversal and guidance fading [Evidence/Prescription]** "As students become more knowledgeable in an area from studying worked examples, having a student complete problem-solving tasks will become more effective"; begin with substantial guidance and gradually reduce it — [S25](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/cognitive-load-theory-teaching-strategies)
- **CLT redundancy, transient information, split attention, modality [Prescription]** Avoid presenting identical information in multiple formats (e.g. reading aloud text that is on screen); because speech and animation disappear (transient information), present auditory information in small chunks or provide a written alternative; physically integrate text into diagrams rather than separating them (split attention); present complementary text for diagrams by audio rather than in writing (modality) — [S25](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/cognitive-load-theory-teaching-strategies)
- **Willingham, editions** *Why Don't Students Like School?* Jossey-Bass 2009; 2nd edition Wiley 2021 — [S31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_Don%27t_Students_Like_School%3F)
- **Willingham ch.1 [Evidence/Prescription]** "People are naturally curious, but not naturally good thinkers"; the brain prefers memory to thinking; problems with too little mental work bore students, too much discourages them, "when the mental work required is at a moderate level, thinking is actively sought out" — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.2 [Prescription]** Skilled thinking requires "an adequate amount of foundational knowledge"; knowledge enables inference and chunking, which "frees up space in working memory"; extensive reading is the recommended way to build background knowledge — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.3 [Prescription]** "Memory is the residue of thought (Willingham, 2008), so if you want students to remember something you have to get them to think about it" — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf); rather than pop-culture hooks, "structure each lesson in the format of a story, complete with the elements of causality, conflict, complications, and character" — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.4 [Prescription]** "We understand new things in the context of things we already know, and most of what we know is concrete"; students focus on surface features (the tumour vs army-invasion problems) rather than deep structure; "provide examples and ask students to compare them" (p. 78) — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.5 [Prescription]** Practice makes processes automatic and frees working memory; tips: "focus on the building blocks making up the problems, space out the sessions over time, and progress the practice material from simple to advanced" — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.6 [Prescription]** Students cannot think like experts (scientists, historians); "a more reasonable goal for students is comprehension of the material already in place and familiarization with the knowledge creation process" — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Willingham ch.7-8 [Evidence]** "No scientific evidence exists to support the theory of categorically different learning types"; "children are more alike than different in terms of how they think and learn"; intelligence is malleable through sustained work; "rather than patronizing students with false praise, teachers can show students their efforts can lead to improvement" — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **Make It Stick [Prescription/Evidence, secondary]** Retrieval practice ("the testing effect") beats rereading; spaced and interleaved practice beat massed practice although interleaving "feels slower and ineffective"; "desirable difficulties": "when students struggle with a problem, attempt solutions, fail, and try again, learning is improved"; fill-in-the-blank outperforms multiple choice; use elaboration, generation (attempt before being shown), reflection; rereading creates an "illusion of knowing"; learning styles and fixed intelligence are dispelled; instructors should "explain why pedagogical choices create difficulty" — [S24](https://blog.apaonline.org/2020/02/19/takeaways-from-make-it-stick-the-science-of-successful-learning/)
- **Make It Stick [Evidence, corroboration]** The Sutton Trust cites Brown et al. (2014) and Dunlosky et al. (2013): rereading and highlighting "give a satisfying – but deceptive – feeling of fluency", whereas "testing yourself, trying to generate answers, and deliberately creating intervals between study to allow forgetting, are all more effective" — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)
- **Deans for Impact [existence only]** *The Science of Learning* (2015) "summarises pretty much everything a busy teacher ought to know about how children learn, remember, solve problems, transfer to new contexts", covers motivation and misconceptions, and has a useful bibliography (Didau, 2015-09-30) — [S21](https://learningspy.co.uk/psychology/the-science-of-learning); search snippets add that it was developed in 2015 with Dan Willingham and Paul Bruno and is organised around six questions (*not verified*).
- **Mayer's principles [Evidence/Prescription]** (Mayer 2009, *Multimedia Learning*, 2nd ed.): coherence (exclude extraneous words, pictures, sounds); signaling (highlight essential words/structure); redundancy (graphics + narration beats graphics + narration + on-screen text); spatial contiguity (words next to the pictures they describe); temporal contiguity (narration and animation simultaneous); segmenting (learner-paced segments); pre-training (know names and characteristics of key components first); modality (graphics + spoken words beats graphics + printed words); multimedia (words + pictures beats words alone); personalization (conversational style); voice ("natural human narration surpasses synthetic voices"); image ("instructor images don't necessarily improve outcomes") — [S26a](https://educationaltechnology.net/?p=1878)
- **Mayer's added principles [Evidence]** Embodiment: "people learn better when onscreen instructors exhibit human-like characteristics as they explain concepts"; immersion: 3-D VR is not necessarily better than 2-D; generative activity: "people learn better when they are guided to carry out generative learning activities" — [S26b](https://u.osu.edu/multimedialearning/?p=11)
- **Dual coding (Paivio) [Evidence, via Marzano]** Marzano's "nonlinguistic representation" strategy (effect size 0.75, 27 percentile points as reported) is the classroom form of the two-channel (verbal + imagery) idea — [S27](https://media.cobbk12.org/media/WWWCobb/medialib/marzanos-nine-top-strategies.6990a445329.pdf); Paivio's own work was not opened (*not verified*).

### Inferences
- Board design rules: board points are short labels/steps and figures, not a transcript of the spoken lines (redundancy); every figure has its labels drawn on it (split attention/spatial contiguity); key terms are named before the explanation (pre-training); one idea per board segment with a pause (segmenting); highlight the current step (signaling).
- The talking-head video is justified by rapport (embodiment, personalization, voice), not by learning gains from the image itself; the synthetic voice is a known cost under the voice principle, so prosody and conversational phrasing matter.
- Novice → expert switch: for a new topic use full worked examples; once the student passes two exercises at ~80 %+ (Rosenshine target), fade to completion problems then to unguided problems (guidance fading).

## Marzano, Sutton Trust "What makes great teaching?", Tomlinson, Berliner

### Takeaway
Marzano's nine strategies (led by identifying similarities and differences, ES 1.61 as reported) and the Sutton Trust's six components (content knowledge and quality of instruction rated "strong") reinforce the Rosenshine/Wiliam core and add an explicit list of *unsupported* practices (lavish praise, discovery of key ideas, ability grouping, rereading/highlighting, fixing confidence before content, learning styles, "always active"); Tomlinson's differentiation model (content/process/product/environment by readiness/interest/learning profile) is useful for adapting to one student but its "learning profile" strand overlaps the discredited learning-styles idea; Berliner-style expert-novice research shows experts ask more questions, scaffold more (breaking questions into sub-questions), give more examples, and read the student faster.

### Cited Findings
- **Marzano [Evidence, cited as reported]** Categories with average effect size and percentile gain: identifying similarities and differences 1.61 / 45; summarizing and note taking 1.00 / 34; reinforcing effort and providing recognition 0.80 / 29; homework and practice 0.77 / 28; nonlinguistic representation 0.75 / 27; cooperative learning 0.73 / 27; setting objectives and providing feedback 0.61 / 23; generating and testing hypotheses 0.61 / 23; questions, cues and advance organizers 0.59 / 22 — [S27](https://media.cobbk12.org/media/WWWCobb/medialib/marzanos-nine-top-strategies.6990a445329.pdf) (table from Marzano, Pickering & Pollock 2001, *Classroom Instruction That Works*, ASCD; the slides do not cite it — attribution from search results, *not verified*)
- **Marzano [Prescription]** Similarities and differences via comparing, classifying, creating metaphors, creating analogies ("thermometer is to temperature as odometer is to distance"); teacher-directed when focusing on specific examples, student-directed to stimulate divergence; graphic organisers such as a comparison matrix and a "how alike / how different, with regard to" diagram — [S27](https://media.cobbk12.org/media/WWWCobb/medialib/marzanos-nine-top-strategies.6990a445329.pdf)
- **Sutton Trust [Evidence]** Six components: (1) (pedagogical) content knowledge — strong evidence; teachers "must also understand the ways students think about the content, be able to evaluate the thinking behind students' own methods, and identify" misconceptions; (2) quality of instruction — strong; "effective questioning and use of assessment", "reviewing previous learning, providing model responses for students, giving adequate time for practice to embed skills securely and progressively introducing new learning (scaffolding)"; (3) classroom climate — moderate; "constantly demanding more, but still recognising students' self-worth", attributing success to effort; (4) classroom management — moderate; (5) teacher beliefs — some; (6) professional behaviours — some — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)
- **Sutton Trust [Evidence]** Practices "whose use is not supported by research evidence": *use praise lavishly* ("praise that is meant to be encouraging and protective of low attaining students actually conveys a message of the teacher's low expectations"; Stipek: praise for an easy task can lower self-confidence, criticism can signal high perceived ability); *allow learners to discover key ideas for themselves* ("if teachers want them to learn new ideas, knowledge or methods they need to teach them directly", Kirschner et al. 2006); *group learners by ability* (very little difference); *encourage re-reading and highlighting*; *address issues of confidence and low aspirations before you try to teach content* ("the poor motivation of low attainers is a logical response to repeated failure. Start getting them to succeed and their motivation and confidence should increase"); *present information in preferred learning style* (over 90 % of surveyed teachers believe it; "no benefits"); *ensure learners are always active rather than listening passively* (learning-pyramid percentages "are pure fiction") — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)
- **Sutton Trust [Evidence]** Creemers & Kyriakides' Dynamic Model lists 21 practices under eight headings, beginning with orientation (state objectives, have students identify why an activity is happening), structuring (overviews, outline content, signal transitions, review main ideas) and questioning (process and product questions at appropriate difficulty) — [S19](https://www.suttontrust.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/What-Makes-Great-Teaching-REPORT.pdf)
- **Tomlinson [Prescription]** Differentiate *content* ("what the teacher plans for students to learn" and how they access it), *process* (how they make sense of it), *product* (how they show mastery) and *learning environment*, according to *readiness* (skill level and background knowledge, from diagnostic assessment), *interest* and *learning profile* (defined on this page as learning style, grouping preference and environmental needs) — citing Tomlinson & Allan (2000) — [S28](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/professional-learning/teacher-quality-and-accreditation/strong-start-great-teachers/refining-practice/differentiating-learning/what-to-differentiate)
- **Berliner/expertise [Evidence]** The Dreyfus five-stage model (novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert) as applied by Berliner (1988): novice to competent are "rational", the proficient performer "intuitive", experts act on intuition "flexible and fit the situation at hand" without necessarily being able to state their reasons — [S29](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/hotulain/Expertise.ROPOkopio.pdf)
- **Berliner/expertise [Evidence]** Six propositions: expertise is narrow and context-bound (Berliner 1990: experts asked to teach an unfamiliar 30-minute unit were distressed and did not perform well); experts have automatic routines (Leinhardt & Greeno 1986: experts took "about a third less time to start a lesson", could see who had done homework and who would need help); experts are more sensitive to individual students and task features (Housner & Griffey 1985; Carter, Sabers, Cushing, Pinnegar & Berliner 1987: experts "made richer analyses of the students' earlier experiences"); experts observe faster and more accurately; experts take longer to represent a problem but represent it better; experts' knowledge is more hierarchically organised — [S29](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/hotulain/Expertise.ROPOkopio.pdf)
- **Berliner/expertise [Evidence, cited numbers, small samples]** Experienced ESL teachers asked 32.2 questions per period vs 24.3 for novices (Ropo 1991); experienced maths teachers used 8.2 scaffolds per period vs 4.3, more often "divided the original questions into more simple partial questions" and led students through sub-questions (Ropo 1990a); experienced teachers "gave examples 2.5 times per period whereas novices did not use this at all"; the author warns the samples "were small and the results cannot be generalized" — [S29](https://www.mv.helsinki.fi/home/hotulain/Expertise.ROPOkopio.pdf)
- **Berliner stages by years [search snippet, not verified]** A search summary attributes to Berliner that novices reach competence after three to four years, most reach proficiency by year five, and only a few become experts; the primary source (S37) was not opened.
- **ZPD/scaffolding [Definition]** The ZPD is "the space between what a learner is capable of doing unsupported and what the learner cannot do even with support"; "scaffolding" was coined by Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976), "Vygotsky himself never mentioned the term"; the helper "tapers off this aid as it becomes unnecessary" — [S30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development)

### Inferences
- The Sutton Trust "unsupported" list is the agent's do-not list: no lavish/person praise, no discovery-first for new ideas, no learning-style adaptation, no motivational pep-talk before teaching (get the student succeeding instead), no reliance on rereading.
- Tomlinson's readiness/interest axes are the ones to use for personalisation (diagnostic exercise → adjust entry point; student interests → choose contexts for examples); ignore the learning-profile/style axis.
- The expert-teacher findings translate to agent behaviours: prepare sub-questions in advance for each planned question (scaffold ladder), give at least two examples per concept, and read the student's answer before choosing the next move rather than following the script.

### Gaps
- The Marzano table is from a district slide deck reproducing the 2001 book; the 2012 second edition (Dean, Hubbell, Pitler & Stone) reportedly dropped the effect sizes — *not verified*.
- Berliner (2004) primary text not opened; findings are via Ropo's 2004 review.

## Age-band differences stated in these texts (ages 6-8, 9-11, 12-15, 16-18)

### Takeaway
The core texts prescribe the same cycle at all ages and say little that is age-specific; the verified age statements are that praise is read differently by older students (as a low-ability signal) than by younger ones, that random-selection gimmicks suit younger children, that review programmes were strongest in elementary grades but weekly quizzes help at secondary level, that "children are more alike than different" in how they learn, and that novice-versus-expert (prior knowledge), not age, is what changes the worked-example/problem-solving balance.

### Cited Findings
- **[Evidence]** Older students read praise after success (or neutral feedback after failure) as a sign the teacher thinks their ability is low, and criticism after failure as a sign of high perceived ability; younger students read praise after success as high ability and criticism after failure as low ability (Meyer et al. 1979; Meyer 1982) — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Evidence]** Adolescents: 26 % preferred loud public praise, 64 % quiet private praise (Sharp 1985); praise delivered publicly can be perceived as punishing in peer groups that do not value achievement — [S15](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- **[Prescription]** Lollipop-stick random selection "works well with younger children, but may not work so well for older students", though seen working with A-level (age 16-18) students — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)
- **[Evidence]** "Many successful programs, especially in the elementary grades, provided for extensive review"; weekly quizzes improved final-exam scores "even at the secondary level"; the 82 %/73 % success-rate study was fourth-grade mathematics; the 23-vs-11-minute presentation study was junior-high mathematics (Evertson et al. 1980) — [S1](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf)
- **[Evidence]** Lemov's No Opt Out clips span kindergarten, grade 5, grade 6 and grade 10-11 classrooms — the technique is presented as age-independent; "start simple" with one-answer, one-sentence questions when a class is new to it — [S3a](https://teachlikeachampion.org/wp-content/uploads/Field-Guide-sample-chapter.pdf), [S3b-stretch](https://teachlikeachampion.org/blog/tlac-2-0-adding-stretch-it-to-no-opt-out/)
- **[Evidence]** Willingham: "children are more alike than different in terms of how they think and learn"; differences are in cognitive ability and preferred style, not in learning mechanism; abstract ideas are understood through concrete, already-known things at every age — [S22](https://www.erpjournal.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/ERPV40_Young-Jones_2013_Willingham_Book_Review.pdf)
- **[Evidence, search snippet only, not verified]** Willingham (2008, *American Educator*) argues "developmentally appropriate practice" is "not a good guide for instruction" because it requires (1) that development occurs in discrete stages and (2) that a child's stage affects all tasks pervasively, neither of which holds — [S23](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/media/2014/willingham_1.pdf)
- **[Evidence]** Expertise reversal is a function of prior knowledge, not age: worked examples help novices and hinder learners who already know the material — [S25](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/cognitive-load-theory-teaching-strategies)
- **[Evidence]** Hattie's list gives "Piagetian programs" 1.28 — programmes that match tasks to the student's Piagetian stage — but see the Bergeron critique of such aggregate numbers — [S14b](https://visible-learning.org/backup-hattie-ranking-256-effects-2017/), [S16](https://mje.mcgill.ca/article/download/9475/7228/30586)
- **[Prescription]** Wiliam's marking scale by student strength (dot per error for stronger students, error location for weaker) is a readiness rule, not an age rule — [S12](https://www.dylanwiliam.org/Dylan_Wiliams_website/Papers_files/Cambridge%20AfL%20keynote.doc)

### Inferences
- Ages 6-8: shorter steps and more frequent retrieval (working memory and automaticity arguments in S1), concrete objects/pictures before symbols (S22), public-style praise for effort is safe (S15), game-like all-response formats (choral start-on-signal, hold-up cards) (S1, S12).
- Ages 9-11: same cycle; introduce self-assessment signals (traffic light after each section) and "find and fix" feedback with error location (S12, S13).
- Ages 12-15: praise privately and about strategy, never ability (S15); use hinge questions with misconception distractors (S11); allow delayed feedback on hard items (S15); explain why practice is spaced/interleaved (S24).
- Ages 16-18: expect expertise reversal within a topic — fade worked examples faster; use the dot-only "find it and fix it" marking; ask for justification/"explain the why" (Stretch It) as the default check.
- These band rules are inferences layered on age-agnostic prescriptions; the texts themselves would say the readiness diagnostic, not the age, should set the parameters.

### Gaps
- None of the opened texts gives numeric age-specific parameters (step length, question count, practice spacing); such numbers would have to be *proposed targets*.
- Willingham 2008 could not be opened; its argument is reported from a search snippet.

## Operational rules for an AI teacher

### Takeaway
Forty-two one-line rules distilled from the sources above; each carries its source id, and every number is tagged as *cited* or *proposed target*. Rules are grouped by lesson stage, with age-band variants where the sources or inferences differentiate.

### Cited Findings
Opening / review (section 1):
1. Start every lesson with a retrieval exercise on prior material, 5-8 min of a 40-min lesson (*cited* S1 P1), graded before new content begins.
2. The opening exercise targets items that must become automatic (facts, procedures, vocabulary) and last lesson's error points (*prescription* S1 P1).
3. Name the lesson objective and why it matters in one board line and one spoken line before input (Hunter "purpose", Gagné event 2, Creemers & Kyriakides "orientation": S5a, S6, S19).
4. Open with a short attention hook tied to the previous lesson (anticipatory set, S5a; Gagné event 1, S6), then recall prerequisites explicitly because working memory is small (S1 P1).
5. Ask the student to predict their score on the opening exercise, then compare after grading (calibration; inference from Hattie "self-reported grades", S14b, and Make It Stick calibration, S24).
6. Return to last week's content every lesson-week and last month's every fourth week with a quiz (*cited* schedule S1 P10; "test within about a week of first encounter" S9).

Explanation (sections 2-3):
7. Present one small step per section; do not introduce the next step until the current one's exercise is passed (S1 P2; mastery learning S1 P7).
8. Keep spoken lines short and sequenced; use more time on explanation and examples rather than less — effective teachers spent ~23 of 40 min presenting and questioning versus 11 (*cited* S1 P2).
9. For every new procedure, show a full worked example on the board and think aloud, stating the reason for each step and the underlying principle (S1 P4/P5; CLT S25).
10. Give at least two examples per concept and ask the student to compare them (S22 "provide examples and ask students to compare them"; experts give more examples S29).
11. Move concrete → abstract: anchor a new idea in something the student already knows before the symbolic form (S22 ch.4; Willingham).
12. Frame explanations with a story structure where the content allows (causality, conflict, complications, character) and make the student think about meaning, because "memory is the residue of thought" (S22, S19).
13. Board = labels, steps and figures, never a transcript of the speech (Mayer redundancy, S26a; CLT redundancy S25).
14. Put labels on the figure itself and speak the explanation of a figure rather than printing it (spatial contiguity, modality: S26a, S25).
15. Pre-teach the names of new terms/parts before explaining the mechanism (pre-training, S26a).
16. Segment: one idea per board block, then pause for a check before the next (segmenting S26a; small steps S1).
17. Highlight the current step on the board (signaling, S26a) and remove decoration or unrelated remarks (coherence, S26a).
18. Anticipate the two or three most common errors for the step and warn the student before practice (S1 P8; hinge-question distractor design S11).
19. Speak in a conversational, first/second-person style (personalization, S26a) — the talking head adds rapport, not learning (image principle, S26a); use human-like gesture where the avatar allows (embodiment, S26b).

Checking for understanding (every section):
20. Never ask "Any questions?"; instead ask the student to summarise the step so far, repeat the procedure, or explain why a step works (S1 P6).
21. Ask process questions ("How did you get that?") as well as answer questions; effective teachers ask both (S1 P3).
22. Pause at least 3 s after every question before hinting or rephrasing (*cited* threshold 2.7-3 s: S12, S18), and pause again after the student's first answer before evaluating (wait time 2, S18).
23. No opt-out: after "I don't know" or silence, give a cue (where to look / first step) and return to the same question until the student states the correct answer (four formats, S3a); accept "I don't know, however..." plus a best try (S3a).
24. Hold out for the full, correctly formatted answer: complete sentence, the question actually asked, technical vocabulary (Format Matters / Right Is Right, S3a, S3b-right).
25. After a correct answer, Stretch It: one near-transfer or "why" follow-up before moving on (S3b-stretch).
26. Each section's exercise is a hinge question: answerable in ≤2 min, gradable in ≤30 s, and designed so each wrong option maps to a specific misconception (*cited* S11; S13).
27. Decide the next move from the exercise result: pass → advance; fail with a known misconception → re-explain *differently* (not louder) targeting it; fail from a missing prerequisite → re-teach the prerequisite rather than write feedback (S12, S15 "further instruction is more powerful than feedback").
28. Require the answer in writing/typing before any spoken exchange about it (Everybody Writes, S3b-writes), then display the student's answer on the board and ask "What did you do here?" (Show Call, S3b-showcall).
29. Ask for a self-rating (green/amber/red) after each section and follow up every non-green (S12, S13).

Practice design:
30. Aim for ~80 % correct during guided and independent practice (*cited* S1 P7); below ~70 % add worked examples/guidance, above ~90 % increase difficulty or fade support (*proposed target* thresholds, inferred from S1 and the expertise-reversal effect S25).
31. Fade guidance in order: full worked example → completion problem (student fills missing steps) → unguided problem (S1 P4; guidance fading S25).
32. Independent practice uses the same material as guided practice, at most a slight variation — no leaps to a new task type (S1 P9).
33. Interleave and space: mix problem types from earlier lessons into today's practice and re-test after an interval; expect it to feel slower and tell the student why (S24, S19 Dunlosky/Brown; spacing S1 P10).
34. Prefer generation/recall formats (short answer, fill-in) over recognition formats (multiple choice) for practice, reserving multiple choice for diagnostic hinge questions (S24; S11).
35. Stop practice immediately if errors cluster — do not let the student practise errors; re-teach instead (S1 P7).
36. Give short "at-bat" style repetitions after a correct answer (a series of similar items) so the skill is performed several times, not once (S3b-stretch; S1 P9 overlearning).

Feedback and error handling:
37. Structure every grading message as task (what is right/wrong), process (which step or strategy to check), and self-regulation (what to check yourself next time); omit person-level praise (S15 four levels).
38. Do not attach "good boy/clever" to a correctness verdict — it dilutes task feedback (S15); praise, when used, names the specific effort or strategy in a separate clause (Precise Praise, S3b-praise; S19 "praise lavishly" unsupported).
39. Give feedback as something to do: "two of these five are wrong — find and fix them", with a location hint for weaker students and none for stronger (S12); grades/marks alone produce no benefit (S9, S15 Butler).
40. Time feedback by phase: immediate correction while acquiring a new step; after the set during fluency practice; a short delay is acceptable for hard items (S15 Kulik & Kulik, Clariana).
41. Treat errors as information: say what the error reveals and that fixing it is expected (culture of error, S3b-error; S15 climate where errors lead to learning); never use discouraging framing (S15, -0.14).

Closing (last section):
42. End with an exit-ticket exercise on the lesson's key idea, grade it, state in one line what will be reviewed next time, and record the error points for the next opening review (S3b-resources; S13 exit passes; S1 P1).

Age-band variants (inferences unless marked):
43. Ages 6-8: praise for effort may be public and frequent (younger students read it as high ability, *cited* S15); use game-like response formats with a start signal (S1 P3); keep steps and spoken lines shortest; always concrete objects/pictures before symbols (S22).
44. Ages 9-11: introduce the traffic-light self-rating and "find and fix" with error locations (S12, S13); random-style "your turn" prompts work well (S12).
45. Ages 12-15: praise privately and only about strategy/effort — older students read praise after easy success as a low-ability signal (*cited* S15); use misconception-distractor hinge questions (S11); explain the reason for spacing/interleaving (S24).
46. Ages 16-18: fade worked examples fastest within a topic (expertise reversal, S25); use dot-only "find it and fix it" marking (S12); make "explain why / justify" the default check (S3b-stretch, S1 P3).
47. All ages: set parameters from the readiness diagnostic, not the age band — the texts say learners are "more alike than different" (S22) and age is a weak guide to abstract reasoning (S23, *not verified*).

### Inferences
- Rules 1-42 are age-agnostic because the sources are; rules 43-47 are the only age-differentiated layer the sources support, and most of it rests on a single feedback-perception finding (S15) plus Wiliam's and Rosenshine's asides.
- The two numeric controls the agent can actually measure locally — exercise success rate (target ~80 %, S1) and wait time before hinting (≥3 s, S12/S18) — should be logged from the first pilot so that the *cited* targets can be replaced by *measured locally* values.

### Gaps
- No source gives a tested rule for how many sections or how many exercises per 40 minutes; "3-5 sections, one graded exercise each" remains a *proposed target* consistent with, but not prescribed by, Rosenshine's small-steps principle.
- Whether Mayer's voice/image findings hold for a synthetic-voice, generated-video teacher of children is *not verified* (the summaries opened do not state study populations).
